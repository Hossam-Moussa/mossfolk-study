Today we're going to talk about an element of the horror trinity that is often overlooked: the protagonist. Most of the time when I hear people talk about horror, I hear them talking about the monster or maybe the environment.  And although both are very important, there is a third part of that triad that cannot be overlooked.  The main character who interacts with those two is equally important.  The way we, as players or spectators , experience the monster and environment of a horror story depends largely on who the protagonist is.  The first thing needed for a horror protagonist to be successful is that they are someone we can identify with. The more it resembles us, the more we can understand the terror it is experiencing.  This usually means 
[1m] that the best horror protagonists are ordinary people like Laurie Strode in Halloween, Harry Mason in Silent Hill, or even William Dyer in At the Mountains of Madness. Even in strange and fantastical settings, you'll find that effective horror protagonists are often quite ordinary people like Ellen Ripley in Alien.  In fact, Alien is a good example.  Think about how much more terrifying Alien is compared to the Predator movie. Now, Predator is incredible and certainly has some tense moments, but it's not as viscerally terrifying as Alien because most of us find it harder to identify with the terror of a burly member of the special forces who, in a pinch, can set nasty traps and launch explosive arrows.  But Ripley is nothing more than a blue-collar miner thrown into circumstances beyond her comprehension.  He is someone we can identify with, which brings us to the next point.  Terror is about helplessness.  It's about feeling fear in a safe place.  This means that the protagonist has to be weak and powerless compared to the forces he faces. A nanny against a psychopath, a writer trying to protect his daughter from a hellish town, a geologist facing an unfathomable cosmic evil , the mere thought of which would drive a man mad.  The closer 
[2m] the protagonist gets to matching the power of the forces deployed against him, the more the story leans towards action rather than terror. After all, simply having a monster doesn't make something terrifying.  The terror does not come from the mere existence of the monster.  It is built around the relationship between the monster and the main character. This means that the more we empower the protagonist, the more we deviate from making him a policeman, soldier, or criminal, the more defenseless we have to make him in the face of the threat we put in front of him.  The more disproportionately small it has to appear in comparison to the forces with which it comes into contact. Otherwise, you'll end up with an action story featuring some extremely ingenious villains.  So now you have a protagonist that the audience can identify with, feel pathos for, and experience terror for, and feel catharsis in knowing that in the end, unlike the protagonist, they will be able to walk away from this experience and return to their safe, everyday life.  Now, once you have that, there are three types of horror protagonists you can create.  The protagonist who overcomes, the protagonist who fails, and the protagonist who is horror itself.  The protagonist who overcomes is the type who leaves us with a slightly more optimistic feeling in the end, relatively speaking.  You'll recognize this protagonist from many American horror movies.  They start out completely overwhelmed and powerless, 
[3m] but throughout the experience, they grow and toughen up, eventually becoming a match for the monster they face, be it a cosmic horror or a fugitive killer. Naturally, this type of terror leaves us with the feeling that no matter how bad things get, we can overcome them.  Therefore, by its very nature, this ending removes some of the terror from the experience at the end.  The second type, on the other hand, the protagonist who fails, makes us feel small and powerless.  This type of protagonist fights against the monster, but in the end succumbs to it, calling into question the only thing most of us use to anchor our lives: free will.  By questioning whether we truly have control or any power over our destiny in the face of the darkness that surrounds us, it forces us to confront a fundamental human fear.  To highlight this, the protagonist who fails is often made to seem like the protagonist who overcomes even the last moments of an experience, when all his supposed control is shown to be an illusion.  This is very common in Japanese and Korean horror, the prime example being Ringu or the American version The Ring, if you prefer.  The last type of horror protagonist, and the one I find most powerful, is the protagonist who is horror itself.  This is 
[4m] Dr. Frankenstein, Dorian Gray, or James Sunderland.  This is the protagonist who reflects the darkest nature that we all carry within us. They are people we can respect and relate to, and we can even consider ourselves among them .  But, as we realize throughout the experience, they are the real monster.  They frighten and intrigue us because they represent that thin line that restrains our darkest impulses.  They show us how easy it would be for us to stumble to the other side if it weren't for this thin layer of civilization that we all carry.  These protagonists leave us shocked.    They do not resort to the visceral fears of the other two types, but rather play on a deeper fear.  They leave us disturbed, perhaps horrified rather than truly frightened, because they ask us to confront our own secret desires, and perverse thoughts are primal instincts that run through our heads at that moment before we say to ourselves, "Oh, that's terrible," and dismiss them.  And for me, this is the most enriching experience.  It is the most difficult to pull off, but when it happens, seeing or playing such a protagonist teaches us something about ourselves.
