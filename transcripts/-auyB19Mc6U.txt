[3m] screen no matter where you look.  The last type of interface, the meta, is quite interesting because it distinguishes between the game space and the screen space.  In games with a non-diegetic interface, the screen and HUD are not part of the game world and the characters don't even know they exist.  For example, there is n't actually a camera following Link around Hyrule that shows a heart bar to represent his life. It's there just for the player.  But the meta interface lies between both distinctions.  A meta element, in effect, is part of the game world but does not exist spatially in that world.  The most common example of this is the blood spatter on the screen that many shooting games use to indicate damage.  Unlike Link's hearts, we can assume that the blood that comes out of the bodies exists in the world where we play, but the splash on the camera is just a meta visual effect that informs the player that he has been hit.  So, now that we've analyzed this, let's return to the rise of the diegetic interface mentioned earlier, because many interesting experiments were done in pursuit of realism. So, what did we learn from that period?  Well, unfortunately, what most of those games discovered was that the result wasn't worth it , at least most of the time.  Some experiments went well, others not so much.  And this is evident when we see how many current games still rely heavily on 2D HUDs.  It seems that certain information is best represented by a bar, a number on the screen, or even a menu list. This is because the purpose of the interface is to display critical information to the player.  So, presenting that information in the most direct way is usually better.  Sure, long and flashy animations like opening your jacket to see your inventory can be clever narrative tricks , but they get tiresome very quickly. 
[5m] Of course, this does not mean that a diegetic interface cannot be interesting or immersive if done well .  But a good interface must be readable first and stylish second.    You know, form follows function. If you truly believe that a diegetic interface fits well in your game, make sure that accessing the information is no less convenient than if it were non-diegetic.  For example, the diegetic health in Dead Space worked because the over-the- shoulder camera allowed the health bar to always be visible, as it would be in 2D.  But on the other hand, Dead Space's diegetic 3D map was so clunky and unusable that most players ended up abandoning it altogether.  And sometimes, the diegetic interface can struggle with the way interface elements have been coded over the years.  For example, is the health information in the bottom left and the ammo information in the bottom right really the best location for this information? Maybe, or maybe not.  But we're all so used to it that trying to radically change it in your game will probably just annoy people, no matter how cleverly you 've integrated it into the narrative. 
[6m] So it's important to stop and ask: how do diegetic elements benefit the game without being less comfortable?  Having said all that, we don't want to sound too negative about the idea of ​​the diegetic interface , because it's great when games pay so much attention to detail that even their interface is part of the story.    I mean , many of us play games to lose ourselves in new worlds, after all.  But while it may seem that flat interface elements break immersion more, the irony is that diegetic elements, if not implemented perfectly, can be distracting by drawing attention to themselves, also breaking that immersion.  Therefore, a simple interface displayed concisely is sometimes more immersive than realistic but heavy diegetic elements, because your brain absorbs the information unconsciously and allows you to keep your focus where it should be.
