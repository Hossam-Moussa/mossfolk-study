
[0m] In this video, we are going to talk about the design process of a small or an independent game. It is the first phase of a game project, also known as the pre-production. I’m going to attempt to break it down in 4 big steps. Just note that this tutorial is not a method. As indies, we keep a form of amateurism in the way we approach game creation. And I don’t mean that in a negative way. Every small team has its own way of creating games. Some start with a prototype, others with a story, with drawings… And many methods are valid as long as you know where you are going. There is more to the pre-production of a game than writing game design documents. It includes art direction work, but also the creation of various prototypes. It corresponds to a form of research, a phase filled with experiments. The early design stage allows us to give a project solid foundations, on which we are going to base the production. A game is born from an idea, a concept. 
[1m] The first step to create a game often consists of writing this idea down on paper and refining it. Some designers start by writing a game design document. It’s the famous GDD, which can vary both in terms of length and content from one team to another. Note that a GDD is mostly meant to communicate information to your teammates. A long and formal document is superfluous if you work alone or as a team of 2. Also, it is not written in a single go: it’s a documentation that you have to develop and extend as the game is being built. It’s also rarely a single text document, but a whole body of files, each intended for different team members. I advise you to always spend at least a few minutes writing before you start making your game. However, you don’t have to write many pages of text at the beginning. The creation process is organic. Your game’s mechanics are going to evolve as you move forward. 
[2m] It’s useless to try to define controls or the balance of the game if you don’t have it in hand, as this kind of task requires a lot of experimentation. Here’s how I proceed: I start with a 1 to 2 pages long, illustrated document. It is meant to give an idea of the game’s goal, its fundamental mechanic, its universe and its target. This concept is but a support to think about the game project. It’s not a rigid frame. At this point, nothing is set in stone. This allows me to communicate the goal of the project to my teammates, and to stimulate their creativity. With that document as a base, we have something to bounce our reflections on. As soon as our concept is solid enough, it is time to validate our ideas with one or several prototypes. The prototype is to a game what a sketch is to a painting. It allows you to gauge the potential of your creation without wasting time. The prototype is a naked version of a game or one of its systems. 
[3m] Each prototype responds to a very specific goal, be it to experiment with the sensations offered by a mechanic or the performances of an algorithm for example. The early prototypes generally help to get a sense of what our first ideas are worth. We start by programming the gameplay without pretty graphics or sound. Instead, we use placeholder graphics, often simple geometric shapes. They allow us to get a sense of how the gameplay works alone, unaffected by the aesthetics. If the game is already fun at that stage, we can go on with the development. If it’s boring however, chances are good graphics and sound effects won’t fix it. To me, collecting and sorting references is an essential step in game design. Be it pictures, text, music, etc. it’s a research-based activity which helps us to refine our concept, but also to establish a coherent universe and art direction. 
[4m] It’s a task that can be done in parallel to the prototype. It is often handled by the artist or the game designer in a small team. References are not meant to be copied. They only are sources of information and inspiration. For example, here’s the reference board I’ve used to design the visuals of the game Crystal Rumble. On this canvas, you can see pictures that are really different to the final graphics in terms of style and mood. I used the gorillas to draw my main character. Screenshots from Mirror’s Edge have been my main source of inspiration for this immaculate futuristic environment. You can also find the shape of fortified naval doors on the door of the ship. At the start of a project, especially if I have a time constraint, I follow a destructive workflow. If there is anything I don’t need to keep going forward, I delete it. The pictures you can see on this board are only a subset of what I collected initially. Cleaning up as I go helps me to keep a good control of the design process. 
[5m] At the end of the preproduction phase sometimes comes the slice, or gameplay slice. The slice consists of taking a little bit of your game and making it look finished. It allows you to estimate the look and feel of the final product, thus its potential. In other words, the slice is a representative sample of the game you want to make. This can help you to get funded, be it in private or using Kickstarter, and to get valuable feedback through test sessions. Contrary to a better version of the game, a gameplay slice doesn’t require you to create a lot of content. That’s why it is still part of the preproduction process: it helps to prepare the production work. In general, once the gameplay, the tech, the art direction and the universe are set up, we are ready to move on to the production phase. I will not describe production here as if it’s long, it’s also relatively straightforward. At least as far as small games are concerned. 
[6m] During production, we mostly add and refine game content, until the game is done. Even if it’s often the most time-consuming part in game creation, that’s about all there is to it: we produce the game based on its foundations. That’s it for today.
