Actually, this game is much more like the multiplayer mode of Dragon Age: Inquisition than a typical Fable game.  This is a dungeon-crawler game for four players where you and your team try to reach the end alive to gather loot and gain experience, which you will then use to customize your character between games, increase your power, and face bigger and better challenges in the future.  So far it's fairly straightforward, but the important twist here is that, 
[3m] instead of the enemies being controlled simply by an AI as in most games, they are directed by a fifth player who is playing a radically different game than our four heroes.  The villain player can choose where to place all enemy encounters, traps, and even the main terrain elements of the level, and then plays from a top-down perspective directing his henchmen.  Each dungeon is divided into several small zones that begin with the villain having some time to prepare the area, and then it's an all-out fight until the end.  Okay, those are the basics of the game.  Now we can finally do our thing and delve into the ramifications of this big decision to let players control the enemies. Think about that choice from a development perspective.  This means you have to create two completely different user interface sets , two totally different control schemes.  You have to create the foundations of each level so that they work from two radically different camera angles.  You need to make your art look good and be visually clear from both a distant overhead perspective and up close. third-person action view. Basically, you're adding a second simultaneous game, simply by reusing some of the resources.  It's quite a big risk 
[4m] , and frankly, it's something James wouldn't feel entirely confident taking in most AAA environments.  But you know what?  More power to them for trying.  But beyond all that, consider the platform it's coming to : Xbox One. They're proposing a character play a strategy game on Xbox using a controller.  This is something that basically no one has done well in a console game, and yet the entire experience depends on people being able to pick up and play as the villain. Here, however, they did something very clever, and something that many small-scale console real-time strategy games could learn from.  They limited to four the number of unit types that the villain could bring to each of the small zones, so that they could assign the four units to the front buttons and use the bumpers and triggers to give orders to the selected group.  This makes the interface much smoother than that of any real-time strategy game we've played on a console so far.  In the few levels James managed to play at, having four different unit types worked quite well and kept things simple while offering great versatility and tactical depth.  But as designers, one of the questions we 
[5m] always have to ask ourselves is how our design decisions might limit us in the future.  Will this system be scalable? Will they be able to maintain four different units that are interesting for top-level players within a year?  This simplified control scheme was almost certainly the right choice for the game they're making, but it will be interesting to see what decisions it forces them to make in the long run to keep the game fresh.  Now, before we wrap up, there's a really interesting design question that arises in any game involving asymmetrical team sizes.  How do you balance the ratio of wins to losses?  Every time the villain wins, four heroes lose.  Now, on the one hand, this isn't a big problem because every time the heroes win, only one villain loses.  So, if you adjust your game to have a 50/50 win-loss ratio for heroes and villains, you'll still have players who win an average of 50% of the time.  But it gets interesting when you start modifying it.  Imagine you tip the scales slightly in favor of the heroes.  Let's say we give them a 60% win rate.  Now, your entire player base as a whole wins more than 56% of the time. There are more players winning than losing.  That's something you can never 
[6m] do with most multiplayer games.  Should you do it? Could it be better somehow? Actually, we don't know yet, but it's an interesting problem to think about.  Do you make more people happy by making more people win all the time at the expense of the villainous player? Or do you try to balance your game to be 100% fair?  Alternatively, you could increase the villain's power, reducing the win rate for the entire player base and giving the whole experience a more intense feel, which might also encourage more people to play the villain's relatively unusual side.  It is an aspect of group psychology that we have barely explored as an industry.  So, yes, the people at Lionhead are trying a lot of bold things.  Whether it succeeds or fails, Fable Legends will be a game worth paying attention to in order to learn a lot about asymmetrical gameplay, numerically unbalanced teams, strategic controller controls, and even how the gaming public reacts to a franchise that breaks its own mold in such a significant way.
