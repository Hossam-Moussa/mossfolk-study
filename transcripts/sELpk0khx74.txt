When we play as a character in a game, we step into the shoes of a different person and perform actions that we cannot or do not want to do in our daily lives.  When we finish, we tell ourselves that we can let go of the character again and leave without consequences.  Because, after all , it's just a game.  But that's not entirely true.  From time to time, we feel what our fictional avatars experience as the 
[1m] emotions and euphoria of this fictional environment seep into our minds here in the real world. Unconsciously, we hold our breath during a tense fight, lean to the left or right when taking a sharp turn in Mario Kart, or cry when a beloved comrade we have fought with suddenly dies.  This phenomenon is called psychological transference or bleeding, since the emotions of the fictional character seep into the emotions of the player.  Bleeding is not inherently good or bad. Some experiences make us feel terrible about ourselves, while others make us feel on top of the world, and some players experience bleeding more easily than others.  For example, Eddie finds himself pondering the fate of a character hours after finishing a game, while his wife can more easily pick up and drop the character she is playing.  In my case, I find myself somewhere in between, you know, depending on the story itself .  But all this means that each person experiences bleeding differently.  Not to mention that bleeding can also go in the other direction.  Perhaps you had a bad day at work and you're feeling annoyed.  At that point, it may be 
[2m] more difficult to play as a character who needs to make peace with nearby villagers.  Not to mention that the game you're playing might encourage you to use a diplomatic or cool-headed tactic.  But really, all you want to do is draw a sword and let your blade do the talking to some unwitting NPCs.  Therefore, emotions can both come from the character and enter us as players.  However, to be fair , not all emotional reactions count as bleeding.  Sometimes we get excited about a character as we would in a movie, instead of experiencing that character's emotions indirectly.  But bleeding is a specific type of emotional reaction, one of empathy, where the barrier breaks down a little between who you are playing and who you are.  Now, there are a few different ways to increase the possibility of empathy in our games.  The first is to duplicate or emulate the direct actions performed by the character.  Games like Papers, Please and Her Story are excellent examples of this.  In both, you perform simple actions like reviewing documents or interview recordings, but those 
[3m] actions are identical to those performed by the characters. They are designed to encourage evasion, requiring players to make decisions or perform actions exactly as the character they are playing would.  The more abstractions you remove from a situation, the more you will empathize with it. In fact, involving your whole body can dramatically increase your emotional engagement.  The term evasion originated in the Nordic live-action role-playing game community , and that's not surprising because they're very good at it.  It's one thing to see a character move on a screen, but if you were to put on the character's clothes and literally step into their shoes, the potential for escape skyrockets.  It's similar to what method actors feel when they talk about getting inside the heads of the characters they portray.  And you know, I'd bet the same thing will happen with AR games once we start creating some with a bit more characterization than, say, Pokémon Go or Ingress.  Another way to promote Bleed is to communicate like the character.  Almost all tabletop role-playing games do this because, as a player, you speak and narrate the actions on

And even first-person dialogue choices can help with this.  But the further those choices stray from the character's direct words, the less connection you'll have with the options.  It's like when you talk about a problem with a friend.  The act of saying things out loud makes us connect with a situation more strongly, especially if talking about it and getting a response feels like a real conversation.
