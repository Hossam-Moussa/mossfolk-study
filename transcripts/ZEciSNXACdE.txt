There is a tool in the designer's toolbox that can use this nostalgia to great effect: legacy support.  So, what exactly is this powerful tool, and how can we use it effectively to create fresh yet familiar content that excites fans?  Let's talk about it today on Draw Five Move. On June 21, 2019, the official Japanese Yu-Gi-Oh! website opened a contest where fans could vote on 20 fan-favorite themes to become a new structure deck, a pre-constructed deck focused on a 
[1m] specific theme with enough cards to be tournament legal right out of the box.  After 13 days of voting, the winner, Shadal, was announced on July 3 .  Shadal, a competitive deck from 2014, was an exciting winner of the contest and fans were eager to see what the updates would bring .  Fast forward to February 14, 2020. The North American release of the Shidal Showdown structure deck and Shadoll's return to the metagame as one of the best decks in the game until a world-changing event shut down tournaments. Clearly, the structure deck had been very effective in revitalizing interest in strategy, and its solid design combined with external support from other releases created a nostalgic yet fresh and exciting variation of a deck that fans were thrilled to play again.  This structure deck is a great example of legacy support, which is the introduction of new support to an old character, strategy, or game that is beloved by fans. Capitalizing on player nostalgia, designers create new options to update the original material for the modern era and 
[2m] revitalize the excitement in the game as a whole.  The form that legacy support takes varies from medium to medium and from franchise to franchise.  In collectible card games like Yu-Gi-Oh, it is usually seen as one or more cards related to an old strategy, such as the five new cards from Should All Showdown, or the whole Thunder Dragon arc type , which was designed on the back of two cards that Kya played in the anime , Thunder Dragon and Twin- Headed Thunder Dragon.  In tabletop role-playing games, we can see it as the resurgence of mechanics and characters for the current edition of the game, such as the return of the Artificer class from Dungeons and Dragons and the Eberon campaign setting to the fifth edition in the Eberon Rising from the Last War supplement.  And in video games, this can be anything from new versions of old characters to complete remakes of games with updated content, as in the Pokémon series. Additional evolutions, pre-evolutions , mega evolutions, and even remakes of classic games like Ruby and Sapphire or Red and Green on modern hardware, incorporating new mechanics and changes since their initial release. Legacy compatibility has three advantages that make it very useful for game designers. 
[3m] Firstly, supporting former fans who had left for other hobbies can bring them back to the game if one of their favorite things is updated. It's quite common in Yu-Gi-Oh to hear former players talk about quitting the game after a certain mechanic made it boring, unfun, or confusing, but then returning because their favorite theme received support.  This happens in Pokémon 2, where games that recall or update old Pokémon that they love can bring a long-time childhood fan back to the franchise.  Secondly, updating familiar characters or strategies can attract new players by showing them something they might be familiar with from other media, but with a fresh, interesting, and cool look and style.  Even if you weren't a kid in the '90s or early 2000s, you've probably seen Pikachu or Charizard on TV, on the internet, at the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, or Blue Eyes White Dragon, who ate cereal for breakfast last summer. Reflect.  Some of these things are impossible to avoid because they are icons of pop culture.  And if you show someone what they already know in a cool way, they might decide to try the game just for that reason.  And thirdly, 
[4m] legacy support poses a challenge for designers.  When creating effective legacy support, it's difficult to strike a balance between staying true to the original and creating something new, exciting, and useful.  Doing it well can be immensely satisfying and attract many new players and, in many cases, a lot of money for the parent company, as the product disappears from the shelves.  When creating a legacy support deck, there are three pillars to build upon, and we will use the Shidal Showdown deck structure to illustrate these points. First, the legacy support must be faithful to the original.  The updated version or additional elements must retain some aspects of the original material, so that fans can continue to recognize it.  The new Shad Doll support card adheres to the same template as its main deck monsters .  Each one has two effects, one when it is turned face up and another when it is sent to the graveyard by the effect of a card.  They continue to support the core of the deck's fusion summoning, gathering materials and recycling cards, and a new fusion monster was introduced to enhance this aspect. Secondly, legacy support should significantly upgrade the original .  Games change over 
[5m] time.  New mechanics are introduced and, as a result, the way you play, as well as the design philosophy behind the player's choices, may change to keep the game fresh.  The increase in power, in which over time the threshold of what constitutes an effective strategy increases as more elements are introduced into the game, also plays a role in this. Good legacy support recognizes these game design realities, and any new version or update is created with the game's climate at the time the support will be released in mind.  The support is repeated, adding tools to those of the original and providing new options that fit with the current style of play, but still give the feeling that they belong to the strategy genre.  In the case of Shadal, the deck had a solid core to work with, as it was a relatively new deck of about 5 years old that could already be played in lower grades on the opponent's turn and had several blocking and negation effects available.  The new support was based on this with Shadal Opalone, which allows for more effective negation and recovery on both players' turns proactively, and Rishidal Wendy, which brings to the strategy the ability to summon 
[6m] fusion materials directly from the deck.  Combined with its new trap, the deck's swarming ability increased, allowing effective access to link summoning and link monsters, providing more support and game-ending options in the modern era.  Third, legacy support should respect the original source and avoid making it completely obsolete.  We've already mentioned that keeping aspects of the source material is a good practice, but the original should still be useful in some way with the updates.  Shadal Showdown included all of Shadal's previous cards , some with alternate art for a nice little update.  And, in particular, the competitive Shadal decks of 2020 continue to use cards that were crucial in the 2014 decks. Shadal Hedgehog, Dragon, Squamada and Beast, and El Shadal, Winda and Construct , the fusions along with the archetypal fusion spells, are all used in the modern version of the deck despite being more than 5 years old.  The inherited support along with its own effects combined to create a strategy that plays a little differently than it did in 2014, but still 
[7m] looks and feels like Shadal.  For additional examples of effective legacy support in action, we can look to the Pokémon franchise.  In the X and Y games of the sixth generation, Mega Evolutions were introduced to great acclaim from fans of the series.  They supported old fan favorites and Pokémon that couldn't keep up with the power of gaming in the modern era.  Mega Charizard X is a great example of legacy support when compared to our three pillars.  First, it was faithful to the original design.  Even with its color change, it still resembled Charizard and retained its fire type. Secondly, it brought significant improvements.  By changing its secondary type from flying to dragon, many of its previous weaknesses were eliminated, and it became what fans had always hoped it would be .  You know, a big croaking dragon.  Meanwhile, the stat boosts and the new tough claws ability created a bulkier, more physically attack-oriented option for players.  And thirdly, it did not make the original material obsolete.  Charizard was still necessary to play Mega Charizard X. And while it obviously wasn't as effective as its Mega Evolution counterparts, the standard Charizard was 
[8m] still a fun option for players in the game's story mode and at lower or casual levels of competitive play.  The sixth generation also brought great support to the legacy in the form of remakes of Rui and Sapphire, now Omega Ruby and Alpha Sapphire.  The games followed the three pillars for effective legacy support .  They were set in the same region, maintaining the feel of the original games in their music, Pokémon contests, and part of the story.  Their updates were significant, as they created more modern graphics and music, introduced new features like Flag and Laddio and Ldios, which were tremendously cool, and Mega Evolutions, and small quality-of-life improvements with menus and other features resulting from years of experience developing the series.  And the originals still have their own merit and charm.  Although they 're not so easy to find these days, if you have a copy of the originals, the music and pixel graphics are enjoyable in their own way. However, for every good example of legacy support, there are hundreds more of bad support.  Yu-Gi-Oh, unfortunately, is one of the main culprits for this, since all the core expansions support old archetypes, and certainly 
[9m] not all of them are winners.  In the summer of 2018, Cybernetic Horizon introduced support for Demise, King of Armageddon, and Ruined, Queen of Oblivion , a pair of old ritual monsters that focused on destroying all cards on the field and making multiple attacks in battle, respectively, but that used the same ritual spell for their summons.  The theme received eight new cards, but had little or no competitive success, or really no success at all.  And in part, this was because the card effects were not designed as an effective legacy support.  In the first pillar, the new designs are certainly faithful to the originals. Demise and Ruin each received two new forms, a smaller one, an agent, and a larger one, a supreme, which share their names while in the hand or in the field for the purpose of facilitating ritual invocation.  The new ritual forms retain the type attributes and some abilities of the originals, focusing on card destruction for Demise and battle-related effects for Ruin. So far, a good start.  The problem, however, is revealed in the second pillar.  The new cards did not significantly update the strategy.  Ritual-focused decks 
[10m] have inherent difficulties keeping up in the modern era due to the large amount of resources they require to use and rarely have ways to interact on the opponent's turn.  Although that would make for a whole video. Therefore, any support for the ritual strategy must address these problems.  The support inherited from Demise and Ruin avoids this, ignoring the state of the modern game to bet everything on its previous strategy of board destruction and slower battle effects , the only new elements being some protection and effect damage to close games out faster.  There are cards designed to summon Demise and Ruin on any turn in this stand, but since the monsters still lack the ability to do anything on the opponent's turn, and the summoning cards are incredibly restrictive, it's impossible to consider them a reliable interaction.  And in the third pillar, we see that although Demise and Ruin remain essential to the strategy, without becoming obsolete, they are not because they are almost better than the newer versions.  Their effects are more powerful than those of small agents.  And while the larger Supremes might be an argument for being more effective, their levels and name-replacement effects mean they are harder to 
[11m] find and summon than the originals. Although they start with great fidelity to the source, the lack of significant updates and overcorrection that prevents the originals from becoming obsolete prevent legacy support from being truly effective.  Legacy support offers a way to update a fan- favorite old character, game, or strategy for the modern era, encouraging veteran players to feel nostalgic and creating the next generation of fans.  By using the three pillars we analyzed in this design process, you can create effective legacy support that fans will enjoy playing.  These ideas can be applied to fan-created content for pre-existing strategies, such as custom cards and collectible card games like Yu-Gi-Oh!, original forms for characters in video games like Pokémon, and new subspecies in tabletop role-playing games like Dragon Ball Z. So, even if you 're not working on a game with years of source material, you can try this with pre-existing content. And, of course, there will be trial and error.
