
[0m] This year, James ended up working on many competitive multiplayer games , from CCGs to MOBAs and shooters.  And time and time again, the same question arose.  Why do these games, in general, fail to offer a narrative?  So today, we're going to delve deeper into that question and explore the challenges of creating strong narrative arcs in competitive multiplayer experiences .  That said, we will focus more on outlining and clarifying the issue than on providing direct answers on this occasion, as many of the possible solutions James found are, unfortunately, under confidentiality agreements.  So why don't we have better stories about our competitive experiences?  It's not that they lack a way to participate.  I mean, the thrill of victory or the anguish of defeat runs deep in these kinds of games.  And it's not that they don't have epic moments.  We've all told a friend about that amazing game we played or that brilliant play we saw someone make.  And it's not even that the companies working on this don't care about the narrative or don't believe that's what the experience is all about.  It's simply that 
[1m] these games present a number of challenges in delivering a narrative, since, in a way, what traditionally makes a story strong clashes with the core experience of these games.  Above all, the narrative is about change.  It deals with the transition, growth, and progression of events.  This directly contradicts the fact that short-session multiplayer games have to start fresh after each match.  Sure, between rounds you could get some incremental improvements with a leveling system or something like that, but for each game to be fair and develop as the designers intended, creating a satisfying scenario, these must exist in isolation.  The state of the world must be reset after each game. At its simplest level, imagine a Battlefield match where the level doesn't reset, where the map starts in the state the previous teams left it. Destroyed buildings, vehicles everywhere, first aid kits scattered about .  This tells a very real story .  When you enter that field, you feel the ghosts of ancient battles.  You see the impact of the projectiles and you can probably distinguish where there was a glorious charge or a heroic last stand.  But imagine 
[2m] playing on that map.  It would be a terrible gaming experience, or at least certainly worse than if the level had started in its original state .  And one team would have a clear advantage;  You would have no way of knowing where to find good coverage or where to pick up the vehicles you wanted, and so on.  So , clearly, that's out of the question.  Competitive games would lose the fine-tuning that makes them great by including even basic persistence in the world. Not to mention the characters.  But what if we took a different approach? What if we approached it as we outlined when discussing narrative in MMOs, making changes to the story in discrete increments at fixed moments ?  I mean, these games are constantly being updated anyway, often adding new characters or locations to the world.  So why not involve the player and integrate the narrative into ongoing events in which the players participate?  For example, you could hold an event where character A and character B are rivals.  Over the next 4 months, you collect your data, take your metrics, and the character who defeats the other most often gets some in- game addition related to them.  A new zone based on their homeland, a new set of cards focused on their faction, or a champion that ties into their narrative arc.  This seems to be going very well.  None of these things have to break the game or even affect 
[3m] gameplay.  Functionally, the designers may have already designed all the content that was going to be added and simply change its appearance to fit the narrative change. So, regardless of who wins, it doesn't mechanically change the game at all.  The problem, however, arises when you take into account the player's behavior.  You see, now you've potentially incentivized something other than winning the game. Imagine you're playing a MOBA and you 're really excited to win, but the character rivalry event I described is happening.  Now, imagine that one of the players on your team simply loves one of the champions of the rivalry event and wants them to win.  He wants to make sure they get new content for that champion.  So there you are with your team trying to win this thing, but that other player keeps moving away to hunt down their rival, trying to rack up as many kills as possible for the meta-event.  And maybe they manage to kill that other character like 10 times before the game ends, but your team still ends up losing because you've essentially been playing four against five the whole game.  This is an incredibly frustrating experience for the other four players on the team.  It's exactly the experience you don't want your players to have.  This leaves few ways to deliver a story in this way as well.  There are ways to achieve this , but they are few and must be 
[4m] developed with great care.  There are other avenues, especially outside the game, that have potential, but the narrative outside the game is never enough on its own.  Therefore, having to build a narrative around a game state that resets without that narrative influencing how users play becomes an incredibly difficult problem.  All I can say for now regarding solutions is that, so far, I think we're looking in the wrong places.  There are other narrative delivery vectors, beyond what we consider a grand traditional narrative or overarching story, that might better serve us to create a meaningful narrative experience in our competitive multiplayer games. Unfortunately, that's all we can say about it for now. Hopefully, this will at least help to raise the question and get some ideas moving.  See you next week.
