[1m] people are much more likely to help in an emergency when they are the only people around.  In fact, statistically, the more people present in an emergency, the less likely someone is to ask for help.  This is known as the bystander effect.  And you may have experienced it both in real life and in some multiplayer and MMO games, such as EverQuest.  For example, if you see a player in need who is part of a guild, you might not intervene to help. Whereas if you see a lone player in need of help, you might feel more inclined to help. But why wouldn't more witnesses mean more calls for help, more heroic actions, more knowledge and skills to help the person in need? Unfortunately, research says no.  But this has less to do with our desire to help someone and more to do with the way we interact with a crowd.  We often base the seriousness of a situation on how others react .  Therefore, if no one else acts as if an emergency is taking place, we are more likely to mimic the crowd's reaction and assume that the situation is not as serious as we initially thought .  The term bystander effect was first coined in the late 1960s following the murder of 
[2m] Kitty Genevies, when the case became a major news sensation, not because of the murder itself , but because of the nearly 38 witnesses who did not intervene or call the police when she pleaded for help.  Which brings us to the idea of ​​diffusion of responsibility.  The more people there are, the more each person will assume that someone else will take care of the problem.
