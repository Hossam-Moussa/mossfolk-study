# Fullerton — Game Design Workshop: A Playcentric Approach (2nd ed, 2008) — lossless notes
492pp, 16 chapters, 3 parts. Steer: playability/challenge/fun focus (★).

## Ch1 — The Role of the Game Designer
- Designer = ADVOCATE FOR THE PLAYER: see the game through player's eyes; easy to get lost in graphics/story/features — ★"they won't play for long unless the gameplay hooks them."
- Party-host metaphor: designer sets everything in place; the experience only exists when guests arrive; games are two-way communication, not dictation.
- Objectivity decays over months of dev → playtesters restore it. Watch: what objects players focus on, where they click when stuck/frustrated/bored; write down everything. Most designers playtest too late (end of production) when essential elements can't change — costs time/money/heartache.
- Skills: communication #1 (sell the game many times; universal translator between disciplines), listening + compromise (compromise done well = creative collaboration), teamwork, process under pressure, inspiration (see life as systems: objects/behaviors/relationships — Wright's SimAnt from ant colonies, Maslow for The Sims), becoming a better player (game literacy; keep a GAME JOURNAL: meaningful moments, why they struck you, underlying mechanics — not reviews), creativity (Miyamoto: childhood hike→lake→Zelda exploration; You Don't Know Jack = high+low-brow collision; Takahashi Katamari = sticky-ball from racing-game brief refusal).
- Games are FRAGILE SYSTEMS: one variable change ripples; fixing one problem while too close creates host of new ones → D.O.A. games. Rescue = process instilled from day 1: playcentric + controlled iterative changes.
- ★PLAYER EXPERIENCE GOALS (the book's spine): set BEFORE features, descriptions of situations the player finds themselves in, not features ("must cooperate but can never trust each other"; "freedom to pursue goals in any order"). Features brainstormed later TO MEET goals, then playtested AGAINST goals.
- Iteration loop (fig 1.8): set goals → conceive → formalize (write/prototype) → test vs goals → evaluate+prioritize → (flawed? back to step 1 / improvements? modify+retest / success? done).
- 7-step process: 1 Brainstorm (goals, concepts, top 3, one-page treatments, test written concepts) → 2 Physical prototype (pen/paper/cards/act-out, played by designer+friends BEFORE any programmer/artist exists; then 3-6pp gameplay treatment) → 3 Presentation (optional, funding) → 4 Software prototypes (several, each on a different aspect, temp graphics) → 5 Design doc (written AFTER prototyping knowledge; wikis > static docs) → 6 Production (keep testing; changes should shrink over time because big issues died in prototyping) → 7 QA (gameplay already solid; now usability/accessibility for whole target audience).
- Industry skips physical prototype → forced to reference existing games → derivative by construction. EA preproduction workshop (Glenn Entis): prototypes "fast, cheap, public, physical"; "if you don't see the team arguing, they aren't sharing ideas." Plummer: paper = cheap ideation.
- Zimmerman sidebar (SiSSYFiGHT 2000): play values first (nongamers, low tech barrier, easy-to-learn deep-social, ironic brand); Post-it prototype with designer as the computer; IRC text prototype isolates game logic from interface; dot-com friends cold-tested; beta community became launch community seed. ★Biggest design challenge = making ACTION→OUTCOME attribution legible (interdependent turn results felt "frustratingly arbitrary" until many revisions). ★Defensive play dominated → standstills → rule patch: cowering twice in a row = chicken penalty. ★"The point of game design is not to have players experience rules — it is to have players experience play"; second-order design problem; play is too subtle to script in advance.
- Molyneux: prototypes "prove things we don't know, not subjective things like 'is this fun'"; Fable 2 death redesigned as losing something you care about instead of 20-min replay (replay = frustration+story boredom). Advice: design = what the player DOES and SEES, not storyline.
- Spector: Ultima IV = choices+consequences; ★Mario 64 = "ten things" simplicity yet empowered/liberated/encouraged to explore-plan-experiment-FAIL-retry without frustration — simplicity+depth combo; Ico = emotional power of virtual touch; Suikoden = rare binary choices that MATTER + castle = personal mark on world (narcissism of play). Advice: learn to program basics, broad education (behavioral psych, architecture, economics), make mods.
- Innovation = unique mechanics beyond genres + new audiences + hard problems (story/gameplay integration, empathy, emotion, learning).

## Ch2 — The Structure of Games
- Go Fish vs Quake dissection → what ALL games share = the 8 FORMAL ELEMENTS: Players, Objectives, Procedures, Rules, Resources, Conflict, Boundaries, Outcome.
- Players: voluntary participants who both partake in and consume; Suits' LUSORY ATTITUDE = "adopting rules which require worse rather than better means for reaching an end" (golf: stick at 400 yards, not hand in hole). Acceptance of inefficiency IS the game.
- Objectives: what makes games unlike film/books/life — a clear-cut goal; working toward it = measure of involvement.
- Procedures: allowed actions/methods; create interactions that would never happen outside the game's authority; controls = player's access to procedures.
- Rules: define objects+concepts, limit behavior, proscribe reactive events; authority is implicit player agreement — break rules = no longer playing.
- Resources: assets made valuable by SCARCITY + UTILITY (cards, weapons, ammo, time, units, turns, terrain); direct exchange (Go Fish) vs designer-placed (Quake).
- Conflict: emerges from rules/procedures DETERRING direct goal accomplishment (ask one player at a time, maze between you and exit).
- Boundaries: Huizinga's MAGIC CIRCLE — "temporary worlds within the ordinary world, dedicated to the performance of an act apart"; physical (code walls) or conceptual (social agreement).
- Outcome: uncertain but promises measurable, UNEQUAL resolution. ★If players can anticipate the outcome they STOP PLAYING (runaway-leader concede). Unlike movies, games need uncertainty EVERY play for dramatic tension.
- Definition: a game = closed formal system + engages players in structured conflict + resolves uncertainty in unequal outcome. (Serious games / Sims / WoW live at the edges; definition is a launch pad, not a constraint. Lack of shared vocabulary = one of the industry's largest problems.)
- DRAMATIC ELEMENTS (make formal elements emotionally engaging): ★Challenge (conflict→tension; rising challenge = rising tension; too great = frustration; flat/declining = mastery+leave — the engagement dial), Play (Salen/Zimmerman "free movement within a more rigid structure" — steering-wheel play; design freedom INSIDE the rigid structure), Premise (Monopoly landlord fantasy — contextualizes choices), Character (vessel for participation + empathy), Story (unfolds, unlike premise).
- Whole > sum of parts: formal elements are nascent potential; qualities EMERGE only in motion (car-engine analogy) → you cannot predict play from reading elements → Ch5 systems + playtesting.
- ★Scott Kim puzzle sidebar: puzzle = "fun + has a right answer"; also "a simple task with a bad user interface" (Rubik's). Fun ingredients: novel twist on familiar, not-too-easy-not-too-hard (PERCEIVED easiness keeps people trying even when actually hard), tricky perceptual shift. Fun is audience-relative → tailor difficulty ladders (easy→hard questions per puzzle; multiple puzzle types per column). Crawford's 4 play activities by interactivity: Games (beat opponent) > Puzzles (find solution, little replay) > Toys (manipulable, no goal) > Stories (fantasy, unchangeable). ★RULE OF THUMB: "to design a good puzzle, first build a good toy" — manipulation must be fun BEFORE the goal is understood (Tetris blocks). Solitaire = puzzle generator via shuffle. Two design layers: LEVEL design (config within fixed rules, needs drama+coherence+difficulty targeting) vs RULE design (invent rules/goal/format; harder; reusable rulesets like Sudoku >> single-use). Puzzle design goal = same flow state as games: attractive goal, seamless rule teaching, engaged feedback, appropriate end reward.
- McGee: elegant simplicity example — Alice jump puzzles fixed by projecting 2D feet-landing shadow (novice aid, no automation); "even the mistakes have something to teach us"; think entertainment/franchise, audience-not-self, "small, different, online."
- Petersen favorites teach: Contract Bridge "many different ways to excel — not all good players are good in the same way → clashes of styles"; Cosmic Encounter = first asymmetric player abilities + simple AUTO-BALANCING; Civilization board = every decision touches your economy.
- FOR US: formal-elements checklist = spec audit grid (any Mossfolk feature change → which of the 8 elements did it touch?); Kim's toy-first rule = our "jar must be fun to drop into before goals" test; unequal-outcome + anticipated-outcome-kills-play = rival contests design rule.

## Ch3 — Working with Formal Elements
- Gin-rummy strip exercise: remove discard → ?; remove draw+discard → no game; remove objective → no game. Elements are load-bearing.
- PLAYERS: invitation to play = ritualized entry moment (dimmed lights : curtains : start button); Guitar Hero controller = VISCERAL invitation (play the fantasy, not just the game). Decisions: how many (exact vs range), roles (uniform / asymmetric Mastermind / team / RPG classes), Bartle 4 types (achievers, explorers, socializers, killers — primary style, switch only when it suits).
- 7 PLAYER INTERACTION PATTERNS (Avedon): 1 single vs game (most digital; needs puzzles for conflict) · 2 multiple individuals vs game (bingo/Slingo; noncompetitive-social; gambling) · 3 player vs player (personal, head-to-head; fighting games) · 4 UNILATERAL (many vs one: tag, Scotland Yard — one informed criminal vs cooperating detectives = coop+competitive hybrid, "wide open for digital") · 5 multilateral (3-6 players, board games tuned for generations; ★social sweet spot worth copying into digital) · 6 cooperative vs system (Knizia LotR, undervalued for adults) · 7 team competition (clans/guilds arose unprompted).
- OBJECTIVE types: Capture · Chase (speed/dexterity OR stealth/strategy OR deduction) · Race (dexterity/chance/mix) · Alignment (spatial/conceptual, puzzle-like: Tetris, Bejeweled) · Rescue-or-escape · Forbidden Act (make opponent laugh/break — rare digitally) · Construction (resource mgmt+trading, player-defined success) · Exploration (usually + another objective) · Solution · Outwit (extra-game vs in-game knowledge). Mixes = genres (RTS = war + construction, split appeal). ★Objective should seem "challenging but achievable" + sets the game's TONE.
- PROCEDURES: who/what/where/when/how. Types: starting action, progression, SPECIAL actions (conditional on state), resolving action. Digital: system procedures run hidden (RPG combat math). Constraint-sensitivity: input device, screen distance → "intuitive to access, easy to remember."
- RULES: define objects/concepts (chess king = abstraction, not real king; ★nondigital games keep objects to 1-2 visible variables since players adjudicate; digital can hide complex state — players build INTUITIVE knowledge of unit values via play, never calculating: WarCraft II knight stats), restrict actions (anti-loophole: king-into-check, Go board-state repetition ban, Monopoly "do not pass Go"; tech-tree gating = pacing fairness: knights need keep+stable), determine effects (if-then triggers → variation + excitement + get-play-back-on-track: Jak mana knockout = gentle penalty that re-incentivizes care).
- ★"The more complex your rules, the more demands on players… the less they understand, the less meaningful their choices and the less in control they feel."
- RESOURCES (utility + scarcity, else worthless): Lives (more=better, no downside) · Units (one kind/many; static/upgrading; finite/renewable w/ cost — ★cost-per-unit balance only findable via playtesting) · Health (dramatizes near-loss; regain methods: medkit fast-unrealistic vs rest slow-realistic-frustrating) · Currency (greases trade; barter possible) · ACTIONS as resource (20 Questions; Magic turn phases; Enter the Matrix focus/bullet-time = rationed power) · Power-ups (scarce, temporary, state-limited) · Inventory (priced or opportunity-cost gated) · Special terrain (triple-letter squares, gold mines) · TIME (speed chess, hot potato; ★"time is an inherently dramatic force").
- CONFLICT sources: Obstacles (physical/mental; dominant in single player) · Opponents · DILEMMAS (choices w/ good/bad consequences: buy vs upgrade, stay vs fold — powerful in both modes). Conflict = designed inefficiency that forces specific skills.
- BOUNDARIES: remove them and the game dissolves (football anywhere, money added to Monopoly bank); emotional function = safety (cutthroat inside, handshake after); ARGs/big games breach deliberately (I Love Bees payphones).
- OUTCOME: zero-sum vs non-zero-sum (rankings, stats, multiple objectives = subtler gradations; coop games; ★non-zero-sum enables interdependent risk/reward dilemmas → p.322 link).
- Lanning: prototypes protect TEAM MORALE (team must believe it can be done); ★Mario 64 analog controls = "stiff digital controls on living characters = turnoff" — feel gates enjoyment; Sims = focus-testing would have killed it; best ideas come from OUTSIDE games (sociology, zoology…).
- LeBlanc (MDA author): X-Com = two small games married + emergent narrative from simplest tricks; Pikmin = RTS embracing twitch; SimGolf = "a tutorial in level design"; Knizia = ten pages of rules + cardboard; ★"if you can't say one critical thing about your favorite game, you're an amateur"; design for the millions, not for you.
- FOR US: unilateral-competition pattern = ready-made rival-contest structure; actions-as-resource = Mossfolk drop budget is exactly this (ration = drama); dilemma-conflict = our "risk a merge near rot vs safe drop" design lever; Bartle types → tester personas.

## Ch4 — Working with Dramatic Elements ★★ (challenge/fun core chapter)
- ★CHALLENGE is INDIVIDUALIZED (relative to this player's ability — Chutes & Ladders challenges the counting child, bores the adult) and DYNAMIC (mastered task stops challenging → game must adapt as ability rises). "Just the right amount of work to create a sense of accomplishment."
- ★Csikszentmihalyi FLOW — the 8 conditions of enjoyment (verbatim-level): 1 tasks we have a CHANCE of completing; 2 ability to concentrate; 3 clear goals; 4 immediate feedback; 5 deep-but-effortless involvement removing everyday worries; 6 sense of control over actions; 7 self-concern disappears, self emerges STRONGER after; 8 time transformed (hours→minutes, or a one-second move stretches). Flow channel = rising challenge matched to rising ability, between frustration (too high) and boredom (too low).
- Flow components for design: skill-demanding goal-directed rule-bound activity (no skill = meaningless); merging action+awareness (ALL relevant skills needed → spontaneous/automatic); clear goals+feedback (musician hears wrong note instantly); concentration (game becomes "a world unto its own" — mountaineer quote ≈ EverQuest player); ★PARADOX OF CONTROL: "only when a doubtful outcome is at stake, and one can influence it, can a person really know she is in control" — meaningful choice without complete control or assured outcome; loss of self-consciousness ("little opportunity for the self to be threatened" = safe failure); autotelic (end in itself, vs exotelic).
- Design questions: target audience skills+level? clear focused goals+discernible feedback? merge physical doing with game thinking? eliminate distractions AND fear of failure (safe environment)? activity enjoyable as end in itself?
- PLAY: freedom within rigid structure = emergent experience + personal expression. Playfulness is a STATE OF MIND, not an action. Sutton-Smith play spectrum (mind/solitary/social/performance/contest/risky). ★Caillois 4 types: agôn (competition), alea (chance), mimicry (make-believe), ilinx (vertigo) × ludus (rule-based) ↔ paidia (free-form). Use to name the PLEASURE your game sells.
- 10 PLAYER TYPES (beyond Bartle): Competitor, Explorer, Collector, Achiever, Joker (social glue/annoyance), Artist, Director, Storyteller, Performer, Craftsman. Many underserved.
- Engagement levels: spectator (design for watchers too!), participant, TRANSFORMATIONAL play (play that alters the player's life; serious games).
- PREMISE: makes the formal system playable (you are Gregor the dwarf, not "a set of data"); Space Invaders premise = minimal but no backstory needed to feel approaching-alien tension; Myst premise embeds a dilemma (two untrustworthy brothers). Premise unifies formal+dramatic.
- CHARACTER: protagonist/antagonist; round vs flat; 4 writing questions — what does the character WANT, what does it NEED, what does the player HOPE, what does the player FEAR. Game-specific: AGENCY (player's vessel) vs EMPATHY balance; free will vs control (Sonic toe-tap = character's own personality; Sims free-will = "a dance between what the player desires and what the character wishes" — responsible yet surprised).
- STORY: in games uncertainty is resolved BY THE PLAYER, not the author — why traditional storytelling resists games. Backstory-between-levels (StarCraft) = Groundhog Day loop. Branching = limited scope + dull paths. Emergent story (Sims scrapbooks, Half-Life triggered sequences, Halo AI reactions) preferred.
- ★Schell sidebar — Two Myths: (1) "interactive storytelling ≠ traditional storytelling" is FALSE: good stories already make listeners decide ("don't open that door!"); interactive adds only the ability to ACT; manage it via INDIRECT CONTROL — covertly limit likely choices; "it is the FEELING of freedom, not freedom itself, which must be preserved." (2) "interactive storytelling ≠ game design" FALSE: a game is a game; paper classics teach faster than digital; study classics then improve.
- World building (Tolkien languages→creatures→stories): natural complement to games; depth = long-term retention.
- DRAMATIC ARC: exposition → conflict → rising action → climax → falling action → resolution. Conflict categories overlay: player vs player/system/many/team. ★In games rising action = rising CHALLENGE; Donkey Kong maps the arc onto levels; climax = player's OWN discovered action → "personal accomplishment on top of sympathetic response" — games' unique double payoff. (Critique: Pauline's jeopardy never escalates = missed formal-dramatic integration.)
- Daglow: Seven Cities of Gold = "a simple concept with few moving parts… compelling if the TUNING of challenge, suspense, and reward is elegant and subtle"; SMB = "just the right balance of eye-hand, environmental+enemy challenge, hidden goodies, ongoing positive reinforcement"; SimCity broke the commandments and won. Enjoy the journey (career advice).
- Muzyka: all favorites "very good at what they set out to do"; quality has a diminishing-returns point — most games never reach it.
- FOR US: the 8 flow conditions = direct audit checklist for Mossfolk levels (esp. #1 chance-of-completing vs bot_difficulty, #4 immediate feedback per drop, paradox of control for merge RNG); Caillois: Mossfolk sells agôn+alea with ilinx moments (cascade merges); spectator design → watch-a-win replays already match; Schell's indirect control = level-design steering without rails.

## Ch5 — Working with System Dynamics
- System = objects + properties + behaviors + relationships; remove a component w/o breaking it = you had a COLLECTION, not a system. Games only understood IN MOTION; Salen/Zimmerman "second order" design problem → craft a POSSIBILITY SPACE, playtest rigorously, never fully know.
- ★More properties/behaviors = less predictability = more choice — but NOT automatically more fun (Set sidebar proves it).
- Relationships: fixed (card hierarchy, Monopoly track) / changing by player choice (checkers) / loose-by-state (Sims fridge interesting only when hungry) / chance+rules mix: WarCraft II combat = (Basic − Armor) + Piercing, ×50-100% random. ★Remove the randomness → fully predictable battles kill engagement; replace all with pure random 1-20 → upgrades/resources become meaningless, only unit-count strategy survives. The RANGE of chance is a tuning dial between strategy-value and suspense.
- Tic-tac-toe vs chess: simple objects + fixed relationships = tree small → solvable → abandoned after mastery; chess = simple but VARIED behaviors + ever-changing relationships = pattern-recognition play, lifelong interest. ★Possibility range isn't more-is-better: Trivial Pursuit, side-scrollers, branching adventures thrive on small possibility spaces — navigating a defined set IS the challenge.
- ★Set sidebar (Falco): 4 properties × 3 options = 81 cards = challenging-yet-playable; beginners DROP color (27 cards) to learn, add back later (built-in difficulty ladder!); +1 property = 243→729→2187 cards = unplayable; +1 option = 256 = 3× harder. "Build a matrix and calculate complexity mathematically… complex enough to delight and surprise, not confound or frustrate."
- Mastermind vs Clue: same deduction objective. Mastermind 6^4=1296 codes, pure logic; Clue 6×6×9=324 BUT adds dice movement (chance levels the field for kids), social information-trading, premise+characters → "family game." ★Chance injection = accessibility dial.
- ECONOMIES: need items+agents+methods of exchange; audit grid = product amount (fixed/controlled growth) · money supply · prices (fixed/market/capped/base) · trade opportunities (open/turn-restricted). Pit = all fixed, pure social frenzy. Settlers = supply-demand bartering + anti-hoard tax (7-roll halves hands >7) + bank 4:1 price cap + production phase = controlled growth. Monopoly = fixed product, $200/lap money growth, auction+trade = market value. MMO = persistent; shopkeepers buy ANYTHING (newbie employment floor) but lowball high items (push player-to-player market); Ultima's self-regulating recycle FAILED (hoarding) → designer-controlled faucets. Magic = metaeconomy: publisher controls rarity only; deck-building = players doing designer's balance work. ★"Every action in the economic system should either advance or hinder progress"; economy = community building ("makes socializing into a game").
- EMERGENCE: Conway Life (3 rules → gliders); Halo AI = 3 impulses (perception, world-state memory, emotion) consulted together → unscripted retreat when outnumbered; Sims = rules in characters × values in objects. Simple rules beget complex behavior.
- INTERACTION STRUCTURES: information open (calculation strategy) / hidden (bluff/social: 5-stud) / mixed (7-stud, blackjack) / DYNAMIC (fog of war) — each attracts a different player. Control: direct/indirect (RollerCoaster Tycoon lever-pulling), real-time/turn-based; ★"core mechanic = repetitive action performed throughout — if it's hard, unintuitive, or not enjoyable, the player stops playing." Removing player control (auto-assign 50/50 workers) = design question: tedium removed or critical management lost?
- FEEDBACK LOOPS: reinforcing (point → free turn; Jeopardy! board control) drives toward resolution + risk/reward; balancing (football turnover after score; Settlers 7-rule) counteracts change, prevents too-quick resolution. ★Designer's job: evaluate pace, spot growth/contraction patterns, know when to apply a balancing factor.
- TUNING order: internal completeness (no loopholes/dead ends; players arguing over rules = incomplete) → fairness/balance (no built-in advantage, no dominant strategy/overpowered object — these REDUCE meaningful choice) → fun+challenge (test vs player experience goals WITH the target audience, "generally not the designer or the designer's friends").
- Will Wright conversation ★: player = designer ("give the player a tool… the world is reactive to their design"); LARGE SOLUTION SPACE → empathy ("if what they've done is unique to them, they care for it a lot more"); game = intermediate model, real target = MENTAL MODEL in player's head, bootstrap via overt metaphor (SimCity box = train set) hiding the true one (playing = GARDENING; Sims = juggling/plate-balancing, or director-on-set); pronoun shift I→he when the Sim disobeys = fluid identification; "I'd rather have a huge possibility space where every player has as unique an experience as possible"; unpredicted player behavior "feels like success." Favorite = Go: 2 rules → incredible complexity, board-game Game of Life.
- Moon: Spades "even the worst hand must be played well" = skill floor under luck; Adel Verpflichtet = psychology of tendencies, play against your inclinations; 14 years to success; develop a CORE playtest group; know when to drop a design vs keep plugging.
- Lantz: Sopranos TV game constraints (deterministic + no ties + unsolvable by funded experts + intuitive for nongamers) solved with NP-complete clustering scoring (Tetris/Minesweeper are NP-complete); "Start with a fun game, put some stuff in it, then take some stuff out"; every shipped game = prototype for the next; "Simplificate. Fail often."
- FOR US: Set's drop-a-property = template for Mossfolk tutorial laddering; WarCraft damage-range lesson = merge-RNG width is our strategy↔suspense dial; economy audit grid → coins/boosters design; balancing loops = rival-contest comeback mechanics; Wright's unique-solution empathy = why open-placement jar beats scripted solutions.

## Ch6 — Conceptualization
- Ideas come from INPUT: live a full life, daily non-game activity; Csikszentmihalyi creativity stages: Preparation → Incubation → Insight (aha) → Evaluation → ELABORATION (the 99%); recursive not linear. Carry a notebook. Train "below the surface" seeing: any activity's objectives/rules/resources/conflict = game material (Chinese characters → hidden-symbol system; origin may vanish from final game).
- Game journal evolves into critique ability; USC "game deconstruction salons" = public analysis debates. Study EUROPEAN BOARD GAMES (Settlers, Carcassonne, El Grande, Puerto Rico, Cosmic Encounter…) because their innovative mechanics are ON THE SURFACE, not hidden in code.
- BRAINSTORM best practices: 1 state the challenge (pare to its core; a player-experience-goal challenge is the strongest form) · 2 no criticism, "yes, and…" · 3 vary methods · 4 playful environment/neutral room · 5 on the wall (visual, group absorbs) · 6 quantity — 100 ideas/hour, NUMBER them (reference shorthand + rep-counting) · 7 ≤60 min.
- Methods: lists · idea cards (random pairs: "nectar giants") · mind map (verbs + feelings around core; EA Need for Speed: Most Wanted did exactly this; also fixes teams using same words for different things) · stream of consciousness · shout-it-out · cut-up · surrealist games (Exquisite Corpse) · RESEARCH (immerse in the real activity: go fishing; watch target players with existing games and ask what's missing).
- EDITING (separate day from brainstorm!): cut by technical feasibility / market opportunity / artistic passion ("if you don't love it now, imagine month 14") / business-cost scope. Keep cut lists. Top 3 → second-level feature brainstorms.
- ★THE "X" (EA): razor + slogan. RAZOR cuts features ("GoldenEye set in WWII on PlayStation" = Medal of Honor); SLOGAN sells ("Prepare for your finest hour") — never confuse them; the slogan can't drive development.
- One-page descriptions → player feedback groups → iterate → 3-6pp treatment. Genres = "solved problems"; feature innovation on a genre attracts its core + novelty, but new territory needs player-experience vision first, mechanics follow. Don't let story/characters obscure gameplay — dramatic elements SECONDARY until formal elements pinned.
- Formal-element question battery (conflict? rules? actions+when? turns? players? resolution time? platform constraints?) + structure fleshing: goal, win condition, SINGLE most important player action, critical-rules-only outline, flowchart a typical turn.
- ★Entis EA pre-pro workshop: prototypes "fast, cheap, public, physical"; CHEAP = no permission needed; PUBLIC = team experiences it together (vs document in a cube); 15-20 min exercises — time pressure "filters out mental blocks"; Medal of Honor levels designed in an actual SANDBOX; acting out game features bodily; mistakes "as quickly and cheaply as possible"; shared vocabulary = alignment horizontally+vertically.
- ★Keeker (Microsoft): focus groups GENERATE ideas, never VALIDATE them — group polarization ("incestuous amplification"), conformity pressure, familiar-name bias. To evaluate: survey INDIVIDUALLY with concrete trade-off choices/rankings ("which songs go IN the game", not "which do you like"). Moderator job: pull in dissenters, clarify, draw parallels.
- Falstein: ideas = subconscious collisions of dissimilar things (When Harry Met Sally orgasm scene → siren creature); Monkey Island insult swordfighting born from Pirates! sword interface + Errol Flynn talk-heavy duels; "no idea can ever be truly original" (Pirates! ← Seven Cities of Gold ← a board game).
- Roper: poker ≈ perfect game (simple rules, innumerable variations, no expansion needed, portable, scalable risk/reward, equal parts skill+luck, multiplayer); Diablo II "everything is random so it never gets old"; advice: tear apart and re-rule existing games.
- ★Holmes 7 rules: design for the consumer not yourself ("give them what they want, just not how they expect it"); FUN FIRST over realism ("needs a smack upside the head"); balance always (every reward ↔ risk, every attack ↔ defense); think big ("subtleties rarely play"); PACING = emotional peaks AND valleys (one-note action = flat); play BAD games (learn traps); look outside games, marry new experience to proven mechanics ("the world does not need another sassy female adventurer with big boobs").
- FOR US: the X razor for Mossfolk spec headers; Keeker's rule = how we read app-store reviews and tester panels (generate hypotheses, validate by individual measures); Holmes pacing = levels ladder needs valleys (easy breathers) by design.

## Ch7 — Prototyping (physical)
- Prototype = working model to test feasibility; rough approximation like a sketch; only fundamental mechanics matter — "if these can sustain the interest of playtesters, your design is solid."
- Physical-first benefits: teams get ATTACHED TO CODE (paper iterations feel cheap); real-time response to player feedback; nontechnical members participate fully; broad+deep experimentation at no cost. Deliberately UGLY art (stick figures) — polish breeds attachment and slows change.
- Battleship walkthrough: paper version ≈ identical experience to the shipped box product → mechanics are the game. Tactile building concretizes abstract rules ("what if the grid were bigger? draw it").
- Up the River (controlled design exercise template): mundane dice race + TWO twist rules = game. Waterfall (bottom board piece cycles to top, boats on it die) = positional dilemma across your 3 boats; good wind/ill wind (roll 6 → help self OR hurt opponent) = player-interaction choice. Method: analyze existing system → set NEW player experience goal (strategy over chance / team roles / negotiation) → modify to meet it. ★Small changes to a tightly balanced system have great impact.
- FPS paper prototype: hex grid + matchstick walls (MOVEABLE = tweakable) + spawn chits; 9 action cards (move 1-4, turn ×2, shoot ×3), rounds of 3 face-down cards revealed simultaneously = simulated real time; resolve shoot→turn→move. ★A paper FPS answers level-design/weapon-balance/territory questions but NOT running-aiming feel — one game needs MULTIPLE prototypes, each per question. Additions ladder: scoring, hit % by distance (d10), hit points, first-aid hexes, ammo, weapon pickups.
- Working backward exercise: strip two same-genre games to what they share = the genre's core mechanics.
- ★CORE MECHANIC definition: "the actions a player repeats most often while striving to achieve the game's overall goal." Visualize/diagram core gameplay (Spider-Man 2: everything funnels to points→Spidey Store; True Crime: complex multi-currency web — more complex ≠ better) to spot unintegrated features + feedback-loop problems EARLY.
- 4 build steps: 1 FOUNDATION (core gameplay only, minimum rules, play it solo to see if worth pursuing) → 2 STRUCTURE (skeleton; ★rules vs features: features enrich, rules change function; "you can add rules without features, never a feature without rules"; rules first, features later) → 3 FORMAL DETAILS (full playable game; ★isolate each rule, test individually, litmus: if the game builds out WITHOUT the rule, leave it out; beginners add too much — design = paring down) → 4 REFINEMENT (now add the saved feature ideas; rank by necessity, introduce ONE at a time, test, remove; "great ideas diminish playability, dull ones add dimensions — you only know by controlled testing"; trust your testers over your love).
- Broken prototype rescue: strip to core mechanics, reintroduce rules one by one to isolate the break. When playable AND fun → "start all over again" (good ≠ brilliant).
- Physical→digital: core mechanics carry over (map layouts reusable); what changes = controls + interface.
- ★Gingold sidebar (Spore): failures = prototypes = artist's studies; two classic mistakes: eyes-bigger-than-stomach (ambitious project w/o proving core ideas in toys first) and WRONG success metric (finished-but-informative small things are the successes; big incomplete projects are the failures). Prototyping rules: ALWAYS ASK A QUESTION + hypothesis (diagram on paper first); STAY FALSIFIABLE (user test early; owners who explain away feedback kill their own ideas); PERSUADE+INSPIRE (Katamari's prototype convinced where words couldn't); WORK FAST (minimize time-to-first-failure; "prototypes don't need engines"; 2 days–2 weeks window, longer = alarm); WORK ECONOMICALLY (be a lazy programming bum, decide what matters: look? kinesthetics? load time?); DECOMPOSE carefully (Go-player cutting: isolate problems into small killable groups; all-at-once = building the actual game).
- ★Garfield Magic sidebar (both essays): ancestry = Cosmic Encounter (modular uniqueness) + marbles + Strat-O-Matic; no bad cards (people won't play them); rich-kid syndrome dissolved in testing — too-powerful decks defeat themselves socially (opponents refuse/handicap) and inspire counter-decks; alpha = rules on cards, played until sunrise unnoticed = the fun test; beta = game burst into players' LIVES (trading, reputations, hunting opponents) — the metagame IS the game; cards stealing cards cut (nastiness not worth it); color weaknesses DESIGNED to force multicolor decks after single-color proved dominant (blue nerfed); degenerate decks accepted as part of the fun lifecycle (build→bore→retire to stud); wording precision lessons ("Opponent loses next turn" misread as game-win); Magic = accidental best-in-class ECONOMIC SIM (arbitrage, embargoes, speculation, value fluctuation on suspected uses); Dominia multiverse = setting built to absorb any card concept. Decade later: TCG not CCG — "good games last forever, collectables come and go"; speculation bubble was KILLING the game (Fallen Empires overprint = survival); rotation formats solved power creep vs new-player entry (vs the two bad options: escalate power or end the game); TCGs want SHORT play (replay with modified deck is the loop); interconnected audience forced authoritative rules + banning (house rules don't scale); Pro Tour = NBA effect (serious minority → far-reaching popularity); Magic Online sells virtual cards (same model, targets lapsed players: they leave for life changes, not boredom).
- Ernest: THEME FIRST ("if I know the theme, mechanics that deliver on it come easily; theme-last sessions are awful"); Lego-brick exact-size pirate ships for Pirates of the Spanish Main; "become addicted to simplicity — it's tempting to fix by adding rules, better and harder to take bad rules away"; borrow only with understanding ("not seven cards because your favorite game has seven"); "write the game you want to play."
- Salen: inspiration from MOMENTS (Ico handholding, Mario DS "pure pleasure of failure"); start from desired player feeling/actions/meanings; Big Urban Game comeback power-up (public dice roll at checkpoints) accidentally created valid SUPER-CASUAL players — one roll felt as valid as days of play, because embedded in social context.
- FOR US: controlled-design exercise = our level-variant method; rule-vs-feature litmus = Mossfolk spec review knife; Garfield's "too-powerful decks defeat themselves socially" = trust emergent balance in rival contests; Gingold's question+hypothesis = how every MossfolkChecks job should be framed.

- Mechanics: Blow's Oracle Billiards (billiards + seeing the future) = "not fun but informative — a feeling I never got from any game" → led to Braid. - Technology: "steal it, fake it, or rehash it"; takeaways = abstract ideas (algorithms, concepts), NEVER the code; prototype in a different language to prevent code-reuse temptation; ★"prototypes do not generate ideas; they validate good ideas or refute bad ones" — testable claim + actionable learning.
- ★Mikros sidebar: paper prototype died when accounting overwhelmed humans ("this game is hurting my head") → software does the MATH, paper stays the VISUALS (hybrid!); build in whatever you know; tool checklist: really needed? requirements? quickest build? flexible enough? Flexibility rules: everything a variable (no literal constants), expose variables in UI as editable text fields (no recompiles mid-playtest), NEVER plan to reuse the code (Buddy the drawing professor: stomp on the expensive paper first — "don't make anything so precious you can't see beyond it").
- ★★Swink sidebar (Game Feel preview — full book in gamefeel.txt): good feel = controlling the game is INTRINSICALLY pleasurable (Mario 64 = "thoughtless joy" from the first seconds). 6 components: INPUT (device's inherent sensitivity: button = 2 states, mouse = unbounded 2-axis; natural mappings — Geometry Wars stick circles = on-screen circles, zero instruction needed) · RESPONSE (reaction sensitivity: simple input + nuanced reaction = sensitive system; NES Mario: dampened accel/decel, hold-jump-longer=higher, button combos, ground/air states → fluid motion vs Donkey Kong's stiffness DESPITE DK's joystick being the more sensitive input) · CONTEXT (★"constraints define sensation": Mario in blank whiteness = wall kick meaningless; objects too tight = clumsy/oppressive/anxiety, too sparse = tuning trivialized, "thoughtless joy numbs into thoughtless boredom"; build platforms WHILE tuning movement; football's skills are defined by its field boundaries) · POLISH (layered reactive+proactive motion, sound, effects) · METAPHOR (emotional meaning + familiarity against learning frustration) · RULES (arbitrary variables adding challenge/meaning). Expose tuning variables → tweak toward the thoughtless joy.
- Control schemes: input/output/AI trio; map every physical-prototype procedure to a control; CONTROL TABLE per game state (new state whenever controls change; keep states' controls similar); effortless > featureful ("gamers do not want to think"); less is more, custom schemes for experts only after testing.
- Viewpoints: overhead / side (2-plane control frees cognition for puzzles) / isometric (god's-eye information access) / first person (immediacy+empathy, LIMITED knowledge → tension) / third person (close but sees more). Choice = formal AND dramatic (information structure + relationship to character).
- Interface design: form follows function (don't inherit Gears of War's interface for your prison-escape idea — derive from what's special); METAPHOR (desktop; backpack inventory; circus → ringmaster=help; beware associations confusing navigation); VISUALIZATION/natural mapping (gas gauge; Quake's face bruising = health at a glance); GROUPING similar features; CONSISTENCY (exit button never moves); FEEDBACK for every action (aural = received/incoming, visual = precise data).
- Cloud sidebar (Fullerton's own student project, 1.5M downloads): design goal = a FEELING (childhood cloud daydream); 2D prototype "not emotional enough"; camera zoom prototype in Maya resolved clarity-vs-freedom conflict; particle-sim prototypes for puffy mercury-like clouds; ★features cut (wind, day/night, terrain) because playtesters cared only about dynamic sky + intuitive flight — depth in the core feel beat breadth of features.
- Engines/editors trade-off: speed vs being pushed into engine-shaped design; level editors (WarCraft III world editor: knight 800hp/25atk/245g+60w/45t vs footman 420/12.5/135g/20t — every number playtested; "if any number were out of proportion, players would mass-produce that unit, making all others irrelevant").
- Perry: free-to-play ads problem solved by CHOICE + reward (XP boost for keeping ads on; 96% kept them).
- FOR US: Swink's context law = Mossfolk jar geometry IS the feel-tuner (peg/ledge density ↔ anxiety/boredom dial); the exposed-variables rule = our tuner panels (already English, test panels); Cloud's cut = permission to cut side features when testers point at core feel.

## Ch9 — Playtesting ★★ (tester-calibration chapter)
- Playtesting ≠ internal design review ≠ QA ≠ focus group ≠ usability test. It = the designer checking the game against PLAYER EXPERIENCE GOALS, continuously from day one. Halo 3: 3000+ hours, 600+ players. Testing cycle TIGHTENS over production (smaller issues later); waiting for beta = too late for fundamentals.
- Tester ladder by stage: self (foundation; solutions to glaring problems) → confidants (fresh eyes; wean off: personal ties = too harsh or too forgiving) → strangers ("nothing to lose or gain by telling you honestly") → TARGET AUDIENCE (spend money on games like yours; compare to market; "excessive detail"). Recruit via community postings; screen for ARTICULATE + consumers of the genre; diverse within target; NDAs fine but "do not be paranoid — 99.99% won't steal, and most couldn't use it"; fresh testers each round + bring back articulate veterans to gauge progress.
- ★Session script: intro 2-3 min → warm-up questions 5 min (what they play, where they learn about games, last purchase) → PLAY 15-20 min (longer = tired), "you don't come in the box" — minimal explanation, let them make mistakes, mistakes teach more than flawless guided play; "testing the game, not your skill"; THINK OUT LOUD (running monologue of expectations; remind gently) → discussion 15-20 min (first thoughts, learnability, "what is the objective?", "describe it to someone who never played", what was missing/confusing) → wrap-up+contact.
- ★Designer discipline: do not respond to criticism, write it down; "your goal is not to have them tell you they love it but to discover what they do not like or do not understand"; led testers will happily tell you what you want to hear; group etiquette rules up front; one vocal tester can sway a group (pros isolate testers for exactly this).
- Methods: one-on-one / group / feedback forms (quantitative, compare; individual forms after group play — "surprised how differently people respond with no group dynamic") / interview / open discussion / DATA HOOKS (engine-collected movement+action data → where players stall). Groups generate, individuals evaluate (again).
- ★Lazzaro sidebar — 4 FUN KEYS (faces watched while playing; 7 facial emotions: anger fear disgust happiness sadness surprise curiosity): HARD FUN = challenge→FIERO (triumph over adversity; ★requires frustration FIRST — "so frustrated they are almost ready to quit, then they succeed" → phase shift; difficulty must rise with skill; best games offer NEW STRATEGIES not just more obstacles in less time — Diner Dash trophy changes next level's strategy). EASY FUN = curiosity/exploration/role-play, "the bubble wrap of game design" (drive the track backward, Sims pool ladder; GTA3 gives cars + plate glass and lets the player discover the interaction); ★players ROTATE to Easy Fun to avoid over-frustration. SERIOUS FUN = play with purpose (DDR weight, Brain Age; repetition/collection = visceral engagement; ★"if instead of rubies you matched dirty broken glass and animal droppings the game would FEEL different" — dressing carries value). PEOPLE FUN = amusement, schadenfreude, naches; same-room play = more emotion; "come for the content, stay for the connection." Top sellers offer 3 of 4 keys. Emotions: create sensation, FOCUS attention, enable decisions (logic compares, emotion CHOOSES), affect performance (negative→repetition; positive→creativity), motivate learning. Plan an EMOTION PROFILE at design start.
- PLAY MATRIX: skill↔chance × mental↔physical. Quadrant patterns: video games = physical+skill; board/turn-based = mental+skill; gambling = mental+chance; young kids = physical+chance (ageing → mental+chance). Use with testers: plot the game, ask which quadrant they'd move it to; convert chance→choice (dice→cards) or back to move it.
- Notes: form = in-game observations + in-game questions ("why did you make that choice?") + postgame question bank (general/formal/dramatic/procedures; graph your emotional involvement over the game!) + revision ideas. ★"What you will really remember is the comments you expected or wanted to hear" — write everything. ≤20 questions (fatigue).
- Quantitative: define hypothesis BEFORE measuring; combine quant+qual; ★stats mislead (newbies misuse units; veterans miss innovations) — tool, not replacement for judgment.
- TEST CONTROL SITUATIONS: force rare states (endgame ×4/hour, jail-with-no-property vs jail-with-property, grossly overpowered player) — "not about being fair to your testers… seeing what happens under every possible condition"; cheat codes exist FOR this.
- ★Fulton sidebar (Microsoft): games disappoint because DEVELOPERS ARE NOT TYPICAL GAMERS (expertise distribution chart: all devs > almost all gamers) — fun-for-devs ≠ fun-for-players (modern-art analogy); focus tests usually too late + aimed at selling not improving; AoE2 goal = nongamer-playable → tutorial user-tested with real people incl. a grandmother who built a nation, hid villagers, raised a (woefully inadequate) army before the Mongols crushed her — vs AoE1 where experienced gamers got stuck; AoE2 sold dramatically more, credited to user testing throughout.
- Daviau: FEEL FIRST, then mechanics to evoke it (tense? relentless? casually involved?); Order 66 design: statistically better the longer you wait (one-more-turn tension) + shoot-the-moon comeback win for the other side; "question everything — business people will pull toward the familiar; it's your job to push back, because you might be the only one."
- Connect Four practice drill: time every game; vary grid (7×6→9×8), objective (4→5), turn procedure (2 drops), players (3) — each change × stopwatch = effect made visible.
- FOR US: the 4 Fun Keys slot straight into the game-testing skill's fun metrics (fiero needs measured frustration-then-success — our hard-level rhythm); think-out-loud + don't-lead = app-tester protocol rules; control situations = our forced-state level tests; Fulton = why Hossam's own "I can do better" reaction ≠ typical-player reaction (calibration vs real ratings).

## Ch10 — Functionality, Completeness, and Balance
- Per-stage testing focus: Foundation = is the core fun (self-test)? Structure = functionality+spark with real testers ("is there a spark? or back to the drawing board?") · Formal details = FUNCTIONAL → INTERNALLY COMPLETE → BALANCED · Refinement = fun + accessibility. "Don't fix everything at once."
- FUNCTIONAL = a stranger can play unaided start→finish (paper: no impasse; digital: controls progress the game). Judgment call beyond that.
- INTERNALLY COMPLETE = no compromised gameplay under ANY condition. Red-flag quotes: "the rules don't say either way," "I'm completely stuck," "you can't do that!" Rules always have holes you can't see until implemented; plugging one hole affects other parts.
- ★Spawn-camping case study (legal-but-ruinous play): 4 solutions with pro/con — spawn points = player count (kills flexible player counts) / one-way force field + incinerate-if-idle / random spawn hex (adds luck) / declare it a feature (fight for camping spots = a game itself). Community mods: 2-3s post-spawn invisibility. Loophole-vs-feature is a CREATIVE CHOICE; variants can serve both camps.
- LOOPHOLES: flaw exploitable for unfair advantage that RUINS the experience; eliminate "without closing down all potential for emergent play." Deus Ex LAM-climbing (proximity mines as ladder rungs); ★Asteroids lurking: engineers' 6-month high score 90,000; players hit 300,000+ within 3 months via wraparound-bullet safe spot — "players are much more creative and resourceful than you would ever imagine." Hunting tips: control situations; instruct testers to DISRUPT ("who can most creatively get ahead"); recruit hard-core loophole-sport players; public betas.
- MMORPG player-killing evolution (completeness via live revision): UO reputation system (red names, vigilante hunts, invincible city guards → outlaws to Buccaneer's Den) → two spheres (PK-free/PK-on); Asheron's Call allegiance pyramids + fellowships (XP-sharing = "more fun to cooperate, less fun to be a spoiler") → default no-PvP + voluntary altar conversion; EverQuest = opt-in PK + PK-only servers.
- DEAD ENDS: stranded, can't progress (missing puzzle item, resource-starved strategy, pit with no exit). Complete = "players can operate the game without any point where gameplay or functionality is compromised"; no game is ever truly complete — enforce a standard beyond reasonable doubt.
- BALANCE = 4 areas: VARIABLES (Connect Four 7×6: center column flanked by 3 each side → every horizontal/diagonal 4-row passes center → battle for center = fast conflict; 9×8 = longer AND less exciting; SMB 3 lives, 1=too hard 10=too easy; slug-Mario boring, rocket-Mario uncontrollable) · DYNAMICS (reinforcing loops → winner pays a price for strategic position / randomness / gang-up-on-leader; ★"when the scales tip at the end, let them tip DRAMATICALLY — never drag out the ending"; Battlefield 1942 tickets = minor-vs-total defeat gradient keeps losers in) · POSITIONS (symmetric: first-move problem → weak-opening-pieces rule, Go komi, Hex pie rule, long games dilute it, dice mitigate it; asymmetric: different abilities/resources/objectives still FAIR — SoulCalibur movesets, C&C Generals three play-style armies, NetRunner corp-vs-runner decks sharing one win number; asymmetric objectives: ticking clock (StarCraft hold-out; AoE wonder = player-chosen clock), protection, combination+role swap with time-to-beat (Unreal assault), individual secret objectives (Illuminati: shared goal + per-faction goals → shaky alliances), complete asymmetry = Scotland Yard: unlimited-resources-hidden-one vs limited-resources-coordinating-many) · SKILL (Civ's 5 levels = same system, different numbers chart; median-skill method: hardcore sets high-water mark, novices set low, balance to median; ★dynamic balancing: Tetris speed-with-score; racing rubber-banding — AI max speed just under perfect human, slows after player crash, "the ideal is players feel successful because of their own abilities"; ★Asteroids Mr. Bill fires randomly within a window around the player, window narrows at 35,000 points = tunable believable AI; computer characters MUST make mistakes).
- Dominant OBJECTS: no super units; strengths+weaknesses pairs; rock-paper-scissors payoff matrix = rotational symmetry (Ancient Art of War knights>barbarians>archers>knights); WarCraft II orc bloodlust (3× damage, direct) vs human healing (needs hit-and-run craft) + expensive clever spells = power vs skill-expression balance.
- Dominant STRATEGIES: narrow choice → dull; tic-tac-toe is solved therefore dead; ★favorite ≠ dominant (favorite = often used but counterable).
- Balancing techniques: THINK MODULAR (subsystems with defined interfaces; change tracking); PURITY OF PURPOSE (every component one clear mission; strip-an-element exercise until the game breaks); ★ONE CHANGE AT A TIME; SPREADSHEETS mirroring game structure, built WITH the programmers.
- ★Pardo conversation (Blizzard): daily builds from day one (vs StarCraft's pre-beta instability); "concentrating the coolness" — fewer meaningful units over many fodder units; hero-centric vision → UPKEEP mechanic (bigger army saps gold income → fight more with fewer units) — invented because tweaking unit costs FAILED to change behavior; renamed from "Tax" to get team buy-in (naming matters!); cut the 5th race (Demons: couldn't fill a full kit without making them less cool) and the Ranger hero (kit overlap with Archmage + Night Elf identity) despite fan petitions; left 10% unit/spell holes open INTO beta because pro players will break what devs can't; postrelease: Battle.net stats programmer hired FROM the fan base; ★imbalance spreads "like a virus" once discovered; dominant-strategy spikes often = community evolution, not imbalance — wait for counter-strategies (NFL 3-4 defense analogy), patch slowly; stats decide perceived-vs-real imbalance but "we're not slaves to it"; designer = "primary vision holder, not primary idea generator — a conduit for everyone else's ideas"; fun test: "you're playing too much, start working."
- Hersch: Taboo buzzer = garage-door opener — the single unique component that carries the game; prototype components must be THE THING (cards must be cards, not a list).
- Kelley: idea/emotion first → what interaction+goals put the player INSIDE that feeling; lean documentation ("roll around on the floor if you have to").
- FOR US: upkeep = model for "design the mechanic that FORCES the intended behavior, don't just tune numbers" (Mossfolk drop budgets vs jar clutter); Battle.net stats loop = our MossfolkChecks runs + review mining; rubber-band AI = rival-contest odds engine (70% player win target already matches "feel successful through own ability"); let-the-scales-tip = endgame cascade celebration.

## Ch11 — Fun and Accessibility ★★ (the fun chapter)
- Fun = subjective but testable: playtesters feel it but can't articulate WHERE it's missing — designer needs tools. Games are voluntary; no emotional appeal = no players.
- CHALLENGE sources audit: reaching+exceeding goals (subgoals charge players up; ask testers to narrate their goals — tangents reveal disengagement) · competing against opponents (listen to trash talk = what drives the competition) · ★stretching PERSONAL limits ("we know our own limits better than any designer; self-set goals carry power no designer-goal has"; players invent subgoals especially when they can't win) · exercising difficult skills (hollow unless ample opportunity to master AND display; reward sticking with it) · interesting choices (consequences or distraction).
- PLAY sources: fantasies (incl. against-ethics scenarios — GTA3 compels without fantasy-fulfillment) · social interaction (emergent layer; players keep games alive after official servers die) · exploration/discovery (trepidation-anticipation-exhilaration; "go for a hike yourself and recreate those feelings") · collection (hunter-gatherer pull, single-hand or years-long) · stimulation (senses) · self-expression/performance · construction AND destruction (sand castle stomping) · story (empathy vs AGENCY distinction again).
- ★Appeal analysis: WoW = 10 pleasures, Monopoly = 6, Tetris = 4 — all wildly successful: "no relationship between the NUMBER of elements and the amount of fun; it's the right combination."
- ★DECISION SCALE + types: hollow (no consequence) / obvious (no decision) / uninformed (arbitrary — "arbitrary decisions kill a player's sense of responsibility") / informed / dramatic (emotional state) / weighted (consequences both sides) / immediate / long-term. Golden-arrow example stacks informed+dramatic+weighted+immediate+long-term = critical choice. Decisions rise+fall in waves, ratchet to climax; "nonstop action gets boring too — the breather is where we appreciate, anticipate, steel ourselves."
- Game theory for designers: cake-cutting = zero-sum, optimal strategies meet, PREDICTABLE = avoid; minimax ("maximize your minimum"); ★Prisoner's Dilemma hierarchy Temptation > Reward > Punishment > Sucker → rational play produces mutually worse outcome = TRUE dilemma; Bocska transporter example ($5000 tech useful only if BOTH buy; with negotiation allowed = deceit/cooperation drama every play) — ★build dilemmas into the CORE gameplay, tied to the objective.
- PUZZLES: conflict engine of single-player; contextualize choices as toward/away from solution; must advance goal or story ("if a puzzle does not enable progress, it is a mere distraction — redo or remove"); woven in = doesn't feel like a puzzle. Puzzle Pirates = multiplayer of single-player duty-puzzles.
- ★REWARDS/PUNISHMENTS: best rewards = useful toward victory + romantic association + story-tied. Threat of punishment > punishment (Thief lockpick tension under "the anvil of punishment"). Steady small rewards → meaningless. ★Skinner/Yee: fixed interval < fixed ratio < RANDOM RATIO (EverQuest's schedule; "Las Vegas is simply a giant Skinner Box"). Peer recognition = the reward no pellet can deliver: broadcast achievements in the moment, let non-winners be recognized too.
- ANTICIPATION: open info = calculated anticipation; fog of war = act-now tension on partially accurate info. SURPRISE: judicious randomness = drama (foot soldier 1-5 vs ogre 1-20 — David-or-dead); all-random = meaningless choices.
- ★PROGRESS: milestones advertised + rewarded; "the small payoffs along the way are often sweeter than the final victory"; EA Hilleman MINI-ARCS: ~1 hour = average sitting; each mini-arc ends in a MEMORABLE MOMENT so the player returns; mini-arcs aggregate into the game's arc. THE END: single-player deserves a real reward woven into story, not a fluffy animation; "when the scales tip, tip dramatically."
- ★Zimmerman LOOP sidebar: core mechanic = what the player DOES moment to moment ("games as action, not content"); new-mechanic-first design (sweeping mouse gestures vs point-click); prototypes with LIVE-ADJUSTABLE parameters while running; then all variables in a text file the designers tune while engineers build around it (the score tables printed!); "Gamelab Rats" standing playtest club.
- ★FUN KILLERS: MICROMANAGEMENT (combine microdecisions into macro; sensible defaults; optional automation; "red flag: testers say too much work/tiresome") · STAGNATION 4 kinds (repetition → vary actions + SHOW progress; balance-of-power gang-up loop → tip-the-scales condition; reinforcing/balancing trap e.g. debt eats all profits → windfall/disaster or redesign; literally nothing happening → clear objective) · ★INSURMOUNTABLE OBSTACLES (Halo door case: designers wanted seconds of dramatic confusion, testers pressed every button for minutes — "I hope you all recognize this as 'not fun'"; fix = second explosion pushes you away + jump prompt + floor-mat pointing = drama preserved, frustration gone; Zelda hint NPCs; detect stuck-ness, help without diluting) · ARBITRARY EVENTS (good surprises welcome, bad ones feel like cheating; ★warn 3× before anything catastrophic + give mitigation options; ignored warnings = player's own fault; smaller impact = smaller warning) · PREDICTABLE PATHS (object behaviors over scripted encounters; GTA3 free-roam responsiveness; Civ's 6 victory paths = replayability; "err on the side of greater possibility").
- Beyond fun: serious/educational/art games need different yardsticks tied to their experience goals.
- ACCESSIBILITY: paradox — the better you know your game the blinder you are; testers must be target-market + objective + NEVER played it; 3-5 per segment (8 preferred); one-on-one (people hide stumbling, copy neighbors); "participants in a test like this are never wrong… if your players cannot play, there is no game."
- ★Sweet audio sidebar: audio = feedback device (BLiX game-over sound so hated players immediately restart; timer sound "jolts you back to reality" from the Tetris-numb state); player-generated music (sound effects AS musical phrases over ambient loop); audio sets the RHYTHM of interaction; "higher quality audio gives a perceived increase in the visual look."
- Shelley: "design by playing"; derive but differentiate — "people want games similar to ones they already like, but they won't buy the same game twice"; borrow at gameplay, innovate at vision level AND gameplay level (AoE = Civ content + WarCraft gameplay + random maps/noncheating AI).
- Hilleman: liberal arts — "the technology will change enormously in your lifetime, but people won't."
- FOR US: decision-scale audit per Mossfolk level (how many hollow drops?); random-ratio rewards = merge-cascade economies (already matches); mini-arc memorable moments = our 10-level rhythm law; Halo-door = exactly the Loop Sort level-5 stranger stall (430s) — detect+nudge without diluting; personal-limits = Challenge Lab watch-a-win replays.

## Ch12 — Team Structures
- Production values grew from 4-28KB games (Space Invaders/Asteroids/Pac-Man) to film-scale; teams specialized accordingly.
- Publisher/developer split: publisher finances+distributes (advance against royalties), developer delivers; in-house teams still run as small companies (own P&L) so the publisher can compare them to externals.
- GAME DESIGNER = responsible for the play experience; ★role-combination conflicts: designer-programmer can't be objective about a feature that took months to code; designer-producer = advocate-spend vs guard-budget contradiction (EA Canada fix: producer designs, a "development director" owns schedule/budget). "Exact titles don't matter; someone must focus on gameplay without distraction."
- PRODUCER = project leader, single point of contact publisher↔developer (both sides have one; the pair = the conduit), schedule/budget/resources/morale, "United Nations office."
- PROGRAMMERS: learn programming basics to share a language; respect hierarchy (never bypass the tech director to ask the DB engineer — "no better way to create an adversary"); goal = programmers asking YOU "when's the next playtest?"
- VISUAL ARTISTS: communicate via visual REFERENCE (1950s fabric scans became a game's palette); artists condensing your wireframes may be fixing real convolution — evaluate objectively; assemble artists passionate about the target style ("don't hire an orchestra percussionist for your rock band"); criticism exists to move the project forward, find what's usable first.
- QA: designer's best friend, last defense; design features returned as bugs = help, not criticism; bring QA into wireframe review EARLY → investment → crunch priority.
- Specialized media (writers, sound, mocap): contractors by the hour — define needs BEFORE they start; speak their language, not game jargon; deep sound-designer involvement surprises you with experience gains.
- LEVEL DESIGNERS: implement+invent levels in the editor; ★"foster creativity rather than make them toe a strict line… their innovations only make you look better."
- Publisher side: producer (marshals marketing+execs, one step removed = sometimes MORE objective), marketing (strongest link to buyer demand; bring in early; box bullet-points = focus device), execs (find out their background before resenting input; if they're "ruining" the game, YOU may have failed to communicate the vision — educate via open brainstorm), publisher QA (acceptance triggers milestone payment), usability specialists (bring in EARLY, not end).
- ★"All contribute to the design": suggestion list, one-on-one creative talks, open brainstorms down to production assistants, present stuck design issues as creative challenges, "say WE not I" — authorship sharing is deliberate practice (matches Pardo's conduit).
- Team building: talent × PERSONALITY mix; check how they interact in teams, not just track record. Meetings: agenda+goal known ahead, prepared participants, review decisions+action items, send notes. Agile/Scrum: small cross-functional feature teams, short iterations force communication; suits games because "the ability to change fluidly is important for solving hard design problems."
- Sloper sidebar (getting in): get ANY job inside first; portfolio ≤20 sheets, best stuff first, no complete designs (stealth-submission liability — publish on your own site to make it "portfolio" not "submission"); "game companies need people, not ideas"; thank-you letters in 24h.
- Team size history (Ackrich): 8-bit ~5 people/10 months → PS3/360 era 50-140 people/24+ months → blockbuster-only economics.
- IGDA sidebar: curriculum areas; teamwork/rapid-prototyping/real responsibility matter more than which school; Wright has no degree.

## Ch13 — Stages of Development
- V-model: possibilities broad+cheap at concept, narrow+expensive later; mid-production = features tweakable, vision locked; at testing only details. ★Early prototyping/playtesting exists precisely so big issues die in concept/preproduction, leaving only focused changes for production playtests.
- Stages (24-month console template): CONCEPT/CONTRACT (mo 1): publisher judges 1 TEAM (worked together before? platform-exact experience? "ideas are a dime a dozen — implementation counts"; pigeonholing is real) > 2 PROJECT PLAN (contract addendum = legally binding; think it through with every lead) > 3 IDEA (least important; innovation accepted ON TOP of a proven genre — Deus Ex = FPS+RPG base + Spector's track record offsetting innovation risk). PREPRODUCTION (2-6): small cheap team proves ONE playable level + differentiating features + risky tech; Ackrich: "if it looks like a dog after six months, kill it"; killed projects get the milestone paid; developers die by overspending against future milestones. PRODUCTION (7-22): execute; ★Ackrich: BUILD THE FIRST LEVELS LAST (kinks worked out, limits known → best-designed levels greet new players); alpha = feature-complete, cut ambitious features to make it. QA/POLISH (23-24): ★"70% of the quality comes during the last 10% of development" — leave time; bug DB → triage → beta (no priority-1) → gold (rest deferred); nearly every game ships with minor bugs. MAINTENANCE: patches for medium+ issues only.
- Project plan chain: GOALS → DELIVERABLES → SCHEDULE (tasks, owners, dependencies, workload balance) → BUDGET (salaries dominate; overhead % miscalculated = developers shortchange themselves; markup) → ★REVISE FROM GOALS DOWN, never "fiddle with the numbers until they look reasonable." Written record of every publisher approval = leverage when mid-production change requests come.
- ★Santiago flOw sidebar: naïve×3 team; chose the SIMPLE project deliberately for first title; 3 underestimated challenges (5 player characters' controls, developing on an in-development platform, 2D→3D "plays in 2D but lives in 3D" = real design change); Tokyo Game Show demo = first real deadline ("you make it or you die"); fix = SIMPLIFY, possible only because the design was anchored in a target FEELING ("if it wasn't simple, it wasn't flOw"); "cut anything that didn't enhance the core actions — if your core game isn't fun, you just don't have a game"; "a simple, elegant design solution is often the most complicated to implement"; know what you want the player to FEEL from day one.
- Swain indie-publishing sidebar (2008 snapshot): own site (Desktop Tower Defense ~$100K/yr ads; Peacemaker press=marketing) / user-generated portals (Kongregate/Newgrounds) / indie aggregators (Manifesto 60% + IP retained) / casual publishers ($50-200K budgets; dev royalty 10-15% of publisher's ~50% of a $20 sale ≈ $1.50/unit; much higher if you deliver finished) / downloadable console (hardest entry, biggest audience).
- Chow: "ideas and concepts are cheap; structure and execution make a game great."
- Long (UO): mantra from QA years — ★"stable, fast, and fun: IN THAT ORDER"; C&C crates = comeback luck factor; Ultima IV "a game with a conscience"; letter from a physically challenged player who could run in UO.

## Ch14 — The Design Document
- Purpose = COMMUNICATION, not writing-for-its-own-sake; "documentation is not a substitute for talking to your team"; wiki ≥ doc; living document, modular organization; 50-100 pages suffices; busy readers must find their sections fast; visuals over prose.
- ★Write the doc only AFTER prototype+playtests ("let the game itself dictate how the document is written"); sequence: prototype → FLOWCHART of the whole game (all paths incl. lose/disconnect) → WIREFRAMES for every interface state w/ callouts (usability-testable BEFORE art, "changes cost nothing now") → then the doc nearly writes itself. Iterative passes (levels ex.: name all → paragraph each → maps → populate).
- Template skeleton: design history/versions · vision statement (logline + synopsis: uniqueness/mechanics/setting/look-feel) · audience/platform/marketing (top performers w/ figures, feature comparison, sales expectations) · legal · GAMEPLAY (overview tied to prototype, description, controls, interfaces, rules, scoring/win, modes, levels, flowchart, editor) · characters (PC/NPC as objects: traits/behavior/AI) · story (synopsis/complete/backstory/narrative devices/subplots) · world (locations/travel/scale/physics/weather/time/culture) · media list (naming convention UP FRONT) · tech spec (often separate; new tech, risks, alternatives, engine, delivery, system parameters).
- Taylor: vision/vertical slice sells execs; ★"answer the ten most difficult and jaded questions a challenger might ask — if I can't, reconsider making the game"; Space Siege difficulty-as-story (cybernetic upgrades = easier but lose humanity = replayability + scoring in one).
- Dunniway: know the publisher's prototype expectations BEFORE building (gameplay vs tech vs art); multiple small prototypes in parallel (controls/AI/vehicles) + separate visual prototype at correct technical limits; "it's not enough to write what's fun — prove it"; Munch's Oddysee: cut transformation+water gameplay late → threw out and rebuilt MOST LEVELS months before ship — risky but right; pen-and-paper RPG books as idea mines; "always ask why; leave your ego at home."
- Indie Game Jam sidebar: weekend "no-time-to-think-just-type" codefests as the industry's research lab (shadow-interface games prefigured Wii physicality); Hecker: industry "too risk averse"; Barrett: "we do violent conflict great, we don't do any other kind"; jams spread worldwide, some prototypes became commercial titles.

## Ch15 — Understanding the Game Industry (2008 snapshot)
- Business literacy = design power ("embrace business knowledge like technical knowledge"). 2007: $43B worldwide; consoles = ~2/3 of sales; 60%+ of Americans play; women 40% of gamers, majority in 25-34 (casual+online worlds); players who start keep playing as they age.
- GENRES = mixed blessing: shared language + market shorthand vs creative restriction ("consider genre for business, don't let it stifle design"). Catalog: action (reaction+hand-eye, always real-time) · strategy (turn-based→RTS) · RPG (character growth; MMORPG subscriptions >$1B/yr) · sports · racing (arcade vs sim) · simulation/building (growth not conquest; overemphasis on one part = system failure) · flight sims (mastering realistic controls, not twitch) · adventure (exploration+puzzles; ★Miyamoto: "the state of mind of a kid when he enters a cave alone… cold air… a branch to explore or not… sometimes he loses his way") · edutainment · children's · casual (broadest audience, eschews twitch/violence/complexity; 60-70% female).
- ★Graner Ray girls-games sidebar: McKenzie & Co researched its market, sold 80K self-published after every publisher said "girls don't play games"; Barbie Fashion Designer's 600K → industry cloned the WRONG lesson (one hit ≠ the market), "pink games" saturation → collapse → "see, girls don't play" redux. Lesson: ★research what your specific market wants; never redefine a broad diverse market by its one freak hit.
- Publishers (2008): Nintendo (1889!), EA (16% original titles), Activision (14% original), Sony (45% original), Take-Two (41% original). Research a publisher's attitude/genres before approaching.
- Economics: console title $13-30M dev + marketing ≈ 2× dev; fixed $50 retail → "fewer, bigger, better" → top 20 titles = 80% of industry revenue → niche originals struggle, weak products canceled mid-cycle. Retail math: $50 retail → $32 wholesale → publisher nets ~$15.80/unit (32%) after goods/co-op ads/marketing/returns; 3-6 month shelf life; digital delivery (Steam) promises ~100% of sale price → "a less risky business → more innovation-friendly."
- Deals: milestone advances against royalties; developer royalty 10-18% of net AFTER recoup; step-up rates at sales tiers; ★watch "net" deductions (creative accounting — define narrowly, exclude publisher overhead); affiliate-label (dev shares costs) = 65-75%. Content licensing: 1-10% of net + fees ("branded products sell whether gameplay is good or not"); console licensing: $3-10/unit to the platform + final-approval right.
- ★Tinsman tabletop sidebar: inventors drive design; pitch by serving the COMPANY's needs ("what are you most interested in reviewing right now?"); 7 steps idea→shelf; 10 rejection reasons (poor gameplay, unoriginal, wrong company, ★theme-over-gameplay "Go Fish with Barbie pictures", no legal forms, narrow market, overdesigned/unmanufacturable, unobtainable license, ★unclear rules — "watch someone play from your rules while you say absolutely nothing", cannibalization). 3 acceptance reasons: ★MAGIC (a hook that begs to be touched — Jenga, Pass the Pigs; "in a bad game it's called a gimmick, in a good game it's called magic"), crossover potential, gameplay so good the staff immediately replays.
- Vigil (QA beginner): memorized console TRC requirements on lunch breaks → kept on; "be excellent at whatever you do and you won't do it for long"; over-redesigning on every bad note = design failure — "stick to your guns" when your instinct is sound.
- Vessella (EA): pods/cells = cross-discipline feature teams; engineers+artists solved design problems that stumped designers; high-level feel vision ("fast and fluid") lets everyone integrate it.

## Ch16 — Selling Yourself and Your Ideas
- 3 routes: job at publisher/developer · pitch original · independent. Most designers climb, not pitch.
- Getting in: educate (game literacy + industry knowledge + tool familiarity; tech skills = hiring edge), play+analyze, ★design games/levels (your paper prototypes + concept docs = beginning portfolio; mod THE TARGET company's games), network (IGDA/GDC, cumulative not transactional), direct personalized email to the producer (not HR), internships (verify real involvement), QA = classic entry (front-row seat to development).
- Pitching: <4% of submissions published; sign THEIR submission agreement without fuss (refusing = "I don't know the process"); evaluate order = team → creative → plan; 9 pitch materials (sell sheet, ★playable demo — 77% of publishers call it essential, AVI, design overview, company prospectus, storyboards, deck, technical overview, competitive analysis); after-pitch cadence (thank-you + ≤1 email+1 call/week); publisher internals = sales/production/legal consensus + ROI analysis; ★"do not believe you have a deal until the signature; do not spend the money until it's in the bank."
- ★Hoekstra sidebar: why outside ideas are refused (lawsuit risk → unread deletion; "newbie ambition" — the New York City to scale with 500,000 characters pitch; risk asymmetry: "why pay for an idea when we carry all the risk and have more ideas than time"); fixes: inquire first/offer idea free, get inside, or BUILD THE DEMO ("games are a visual business").
- Miller (Apogee/3D Realms): shareware self-publishing → financial independence → ★OWN YOUR IP ("a developer's only chance at long-term success"; Max Payne IP sold $45M); Max Payne deliberately picked what Tomb Raider DIDN'T do well — "if you follow in other people's footsteps, you'll never be a leader."
- Rubyor: QA → PR → community manager → design, each chosen deliberately as design education.
