## Ch1 Psychopathology of Everyday Things
- "Norman doors": doors that need signs = bad design. Post-office trap story: beauty over utility (invisible hinges) → pushed hinge side. Design should indicate operation WITHOUT signs or trial-and-error.
- Two top characteristics of good design: DISCOVERABILITY (what actions are possible, where, how) + UNDERSTANDING (what does it mean, how is it used).
- All artificial things are designed (incl. services, rules, org structures). Fields: industrial design (form/material), interaction design (understandability/usability), experience design (emotional impact). HCD = philosophy + process that cuts across all three (Table 1.1).
- Machines: rigid rules, no common sense, rules known only to machine+designer; when people fail, people get blamed. REVERSE THE BLAME: the design is at fault. Machines must understand people, not vice versa. Engineers design "too logically" — for people as they wish them to be, not as they are. Even experts err → design assuming error. Three Mile Island: control rooms so bad error was inevitable; Norman's turning point.
- HCD: needs/capabilities/behavior first; understanding via OBSERVATION (people unaware of their true needs); avoid specifying the problem too early — iterate rapid tests, each test revises both approach AND problem definition.
- Experience matters (determines how people REMEMBER interactions); cognition and emotion intertwined. Positive = control, mastery, pride; negative = confusion, frustration, anger. Design attention must go to WHEN THINGS GO WRONG (where collaboration feels wonderful if the machine highlights the problem and the person can act).
- 6 fundamental principles of interaction: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings, feedback + CONCEPTUAL MODEL (most important, provides true understanding).
- AFFORDANCE = relationship between object's properties and agent's capabilities (chair affords sitting; affords lifting only for the strong). Not a property — a relationship. Anti-affordance = prevention (glass blocks passage but invisibly → birds/people hit it). Affordances/anti-affordances must be PERCEIVABLE or need signaling. From J.J. Gibson (direct perception, "information pickup"; the hearing-aid-off story). Visible affordances = strong clues: plate affords push, knob affords turn, slot affords insert.
- SIGNIFIER = any perceivable mark/sound that communicates appropriate behavior — WHERE the action happens (affordance = what's POSSIBLE; signifier = how you DISCOVER it). Signifiers matter more to designers than affordances. Can be deliberate (PUSH sign) or accidental (trail through snow; crowd on platform = train not gone; bookmark = place AND remaining pages — ebooks lose this unless designed). Misleading signifiers exist (accidental or purposeful — games use them as challenge). Doors/sinks case studies: Amtrak sliding door handle signifies pull but needs rotate+slide; London hotel sink with push-stopper = no signifier + faulty decision (dirty your clean hands).

## Ch1 (cont.)
- Accidental affordances → strong signifiers (KAIST flat wall-top collects cups: one discarded cup = permission signal). Misleading signifier done right: rubber pipes across park road (apparent anti-affordance blocks the public, insiders drive through).
- Summary trio: affordances = possible interactions (some perceivable); perceived affordances often act as signifiers but can be ambiguous; signifiers SIGNAL what/how and must be perceivable. Hand-lettered signs taped to products = diagnostic of bad design.
- Socratic designer/mentor dialogue: "I added affordances" → no, the swipes were always possible; you added SIGNIFIERS (arrows/icons). Communication is the key to design; key to communication is the signifier.
- MAPPING: relationship between two sets (switches↔lights). Natural mapping = spatial analogy → immediate understanding (move control up = thing up; arrange switches in the lights' pattern; Mercedes seat-shaped seat control). Steering: works because of a compelling conceptual model (accuracy of the model doesn't matter — usefulness does; tiller, tank tracks, wheelchair all fine). Cultural/biological: up = more. Gestalt grouping + proximity: related controls together, control near its target. Some "natural" is culture-specific (Ch3 time).
- FEEDBACK: communicate the result of action; must be IMMEDIATE (0.1s delay already disconcerting; long delay → people give up) and INFORMATIVE. Poor feedback worse than none (distracting, uninformative). TOO MUCH feedback = backseat driver → people disable everything → critical signals missed (3am dishwasher beep; hospitals/cockpits turning off alarms during emergencies). Cheap single light/beep encoding multiple states = unlearnable. Feedback must be planned, prioritized, unobtrusive-for-minor / attention-capturing-for-important.
- CONCEPTUAL MODEL: simplified explanation of how it works; needn't be accurate, must be USEFUL (files/folders metaphor; cloud breaks the model when offline). Multiple models per device (driver vs technician vs designer). Mental model = the user's in-head model, often wrong, built from experience. Scissors = perfect self-explaining object (holes afford+signify+constrain; insensitive to wrong fingers). Digital watch (5 buttons, modes, press-vs-hold) = no model possible without manual. GOOD MODEL lets you PREDICT effects and recover when things go wrong; without it we operate by rote and can't handle novelty.
- Fridge story (GE two-control): controls suggest false model (2 thermostats); truth = 1 thermostat + air-split valve; interacting controls + 24h feedback delay = unusable ("I shouldn't need a lab notebook to set my fridge"). GE still sells it. Fix = either true independent controls or a chip that makes the false model TRUE.
- SYSTEM IMAGE: designer's model → product; user's model comes ONLY from the system image (physical structure + docs + website + ads). Designer can't talk to users; the system image carries the whole burden of communication. Incoherent/contradictory image → trouble.
- Paradox of Technology: each added capability adds complexity (watch history: stem-wind → multifunction platform → people flee to phones); more functions, less space for controls/signifiers; standards are the exit but slow to agree. Technology that simplifies life by functions complicates it by learnability.
- Design challenge: multidisciplinary conflict (marketing/engineering/manufacturing/support/design all right); successful product satisfies ALL; put all constituencies in the room at once. Store-buyer criteria ≠ home-user criteria ≠ repair criteria.

## Ch2 Psychology of Everyday Actions
- Filing-cabinet landlady: "I'm so bad at mechanical things" — no, the MACHINE is bad with people. Two failures: no idea what to do when the plan fails + false self-blame. Expert repertoire: conceptual model → plan → modify → brute force → bang it (and invent a post-hoc story for why it worked).
- GULF OF EXECUTION (how do I operate it?) bridged by signifiers, constraints, mappings, conceptual model. GULF OF EVALUATION (what happened? did it work?) bridged by feedback + conceptual model. People blame themselves at simple devices ("I'm stupid") and give up on complex-looking ones ("I'm incapable") — both wrong; the design is at fault.
- SEVEN STAGES OF ACTION: Goal → execution: Plan → Specify → Perform → evaluation: Perceive → Interpret → Compare. Skilled = subconscious (think "left", the turn happens); learning = each component conscious. Multiple feedback loops; goals→subgoals; activity can start top-down (goal-driven) or bottom-up (data/event-driven: dimming light → "get more light").
- Root cause analysis: ask WHY up the goal chain (satisfy hunger→eat→cook→read recipe→more light). Levitt's drill: they don't want the quarter-inch drill, nor even the hole — they want shelves installed, actually books stored (→ e-books). Incremental innovation = fix the gulfs; RADICAL innovation = reconsider the real goal.
- Opportunistic behavior: most everyday goals aren't planned — circumstances trigger them; less effort, more interest. Self-triggering trick: ask someone to set you a deadline (external driver by design).
- Most thought is SUBCONSCIOUS (finger-wiggle: the how is hidden). Overlearning → effortless automatic skill. Known answers just "appear" (Beethoven's phone number rejected instantly; doorknob-3-houses-ago takes slow reconstruction). Technology as memory extension (nobody knows phone numbers).

## Ch2 (cont.)
- Declarative memory (facts) vs procedural memory (doorknob-3-houses-ago: answer by IMAGINING the activity — carrying packages to the door). Overlearned skills automatic; conscious attention only for the difficult/unexpected.
- Cognition makes sense of the world; EMOTION assigns value (safe/dangerous, good/bad). No emotion → can't choose; no cognition → dysfunctional. Logic/reason are used AFTER the fact to justify decisions. Subconscious: fast, automatic, pattern-matching, generalizes from few examples, biased toward regularity, weak at symbolic multi-step reasoning. Conscious: slow, labored, compares alternatives, formal logic (Table 2.1: multiple vs limited resources; skilled behavior vs novel situations/danger/things going wrong).
- Emotion is biochemical bias: threat hormones → focus + tense muscles; calm → relaxed muscles, brain biased toward EXPLORATION and creativity (notices changes, connects distant ideas). Positive state = creative but scatterbrained at extreme; negative state = focused but tunnel-vision at extreme. BOTH useful. (= our tuning insight: stress narrows, fun broadens.)
- THREE LEVELS OF PROCESSING (from Emotional Design):
  - VISCERAL ("lizard brain"): instant good/bad judgment, genetic (fear of heights, dark, noise; like sweet), no cause assigned, reads/controls musculature (tense=negative). For designers = STYLE/appearance/sound/feel: attraction or repulsion, nothing to do with usability. Precursors to emotion. Engineers dismiss it but love their own pretty tools.
  - BEHAVIORAL: learned skills, pattern-triggered, subconscious execution (think goal, details happen; speaking before knowing what you'll say). KEY: every action carries an EXPECTATION → hope/fear before, relief/despair after. Feedback = how expectations resolve; lack of feedback = loss of control feeling. Emotional system responds to CHANGES of state (bad→less-bad reads positive; great→good reads negative).
  - REFLECTIVE: conscious, slow, after-the-fact; assigns CAUSES → guilt/pride (self-cause), blame/praise (other-cause). Highest emotions; drives MEMORY of the experience → recommendations. "Reflective memories more important than reality": attractive things work better (visceral positives outweigh behavioral pain in retrospect); vacations remembered fondly despite diary evidence; prestige brands bank on it. One nasty service experience can poison everything.
- Design must hit all 3 levels; enjoyment requires all three. Amusement parks: visceral+behavioral fear with reflective reassurance. Emotion-vs-cognition order: both directions happen (noise→flee→"I must be afraid"; dark alley imagined threats→flee).
- Seven stages × three levels: visceral = calm/anxiety at perceive/perform ends; behavioral = hope/fear (execution) + relief/despair (evaluation); reflective = goal-setting + final compare → satisfaction/pride/blame/anger.
- FLOW (Csikszentmihalyi, in Norman's terms): behavioral-level immersion; challenge slightly exceeds skill; too easy → expectations met trivially → apathy/boredom; too hard → failed expectations → frustration/anxiety/helplessness; flow = constant tension + continual progress, hours pass unnoticed.
- People as storytellers: we force causal stories on events (conceptual models ARE stories); two successive events get a causal link even when unrelated; no feedback after an action → "didn't register" → repeat harder (burning-building inward doors → deaths → panic-bar law = affordance+signifier fix for real behavior). Delayed electronic feedback → repeated presses → intensified/canceled actions.
- Feedback timing rules: respond within 0.1s; long operations: hourglass/progress bar; UNDERPREDICT durations (show the longest estimate) so expectations are exceeded.
- Thermostat folk theory (valve model: max setting = faster heat) is wrong — it's an on/off switch; max setting only overshoots and wastes energy. People can't know: no aids to understanding. Nest fix: shows state (color = heating/cooling), prediction ("will be 72° in 20 min"), learns patterns, app charts → builds TWO conceptual models (device + home energy).
- Blame attribution: own misfortune → environment; others' misfortune → their personality (Tom the office terror both ways); own success → ability; others' success → luck. BUT with everyday things people falsely blame THEMSELVES — and since everyone does, a "conspiracy of silence" hides design faults (Return/Enter story: everyone lost work, nobody reported it — "we just erred"; designer: "did you read the manual?").
- LEARNED HELPLESSNESS: repeated failure → "I can't" → stop trying → self-fulfilling prophecy; everyday design + math curricula practically TEACH it ("taught helplessness"; math phobia = each lesson assumes the last, falling behind compounds).
- Positive psychology counter: replace "failure" with LEARNING EXPERIENCE; scientists and IDEO "fail often, fail fast"; designers who never fail aren't trying.
- Designer's credo on errors: don't blame users; difficulties = signifiers of where to improve; ELIMINATE error messages → help & guidance; let people correct in place, never start over; assume partial correctness. "Human error" should be called SYSTEM/DESIGN error ("why was the system designed so one act by one person causes calamity?"); interaction should be collaboration — machines must accommodate people, not vice versa; people are interrupted constantly, bad at precision, good at flexibility/creativity. The hard part of design = when things DON'T go as planned.
- Microsoft calendar as hero: accepts any date/time/phone format ("a week from Thursday") — fussiness is programmer laziness, not machine need.
- Seven questions users must always answer (feedforward for execution = signifiers/constraints/mappings/conceptual model; feedback for evaluation): What do I want? What are alternatives? What can I do now? How? What happened? What does it mean? Am I done?
- THE 7 FUNDAMENTAL DESIGN PRINCIPLES: 1 Discoverability, 2 Feedback, 3 Conceptual model, 4 Affordances, 5 Signifiers, 6 Mappings, 7 Constraints.
- "Don't criticize unless you can do better"; bad design often = poor communication across the chain (shower: designer→contractor→plumber never met).

## Ch3 Knowledge in the Head and in the World (start)
- Saab key-out-needs-reverse: pure knowledge-in-the-head, invisible — without the note, stuck forever. We function with incomplete/ambiguous/wrong knowledge by COMBINING knowledge in the head + knowledge in the world.

## Ch3 (cont.)
- Penny study (Nickerson & Adams): <half of students pick the real penny — yet everyone uses money fine. Precise behavior from imprecise knowledge, 4 reasons: (1) knowledge is in head AND world; (2) great precision not required — only enough to DISTINGUISH the right choice from alternatives; (3) natural/physical constraints in the world; (4) learned cultural constraints in the head. People also deliberately structure their environment (piles on desks = importance signals) and hide ignorance by copying others.
- Typing: hunt-and-peck = knowledge in world (slow, caps speed); touch-typing course = moving knowledge into the head → speed, accuracy, lower mental load. TRADEOFF theme: world-knowledge = no learning but slower/dependent; head-knowledge = learning cost but efficient.
- Declarative (knowledge OF — facts/rules; easy to write/teach; can be false and still held) vs procedural (knowledge HOW — skills; subconscious, behavioral level; teach by demonstration, learn by practice).
- New-coin fiascos (US Susan B. Anthony vs quarter; UK £1 vs 5p; France 10-franc vs half-franc, canceled in 9 weeks): people keep only PARTIAL descriptions, precise enough to discriminate among EXISTING items; new item colliding with old descriptions → chaos. $20 vs $1 bills NOT confused: Americans discriminate bills by printed numbers, not size (foreigners who use size/color DO confuse US bills). Newcomers/children had no UK coin problem (fresh description sets). Design principle interacting with messy reality; politically-charged rollouts get no adjustment time.
- Constraints simplify memory: epic poetry (Lord's "singer of tales") is RE-CREATED under joint constraints (rhyme+rhythm+meter+story), not rote-memorized; "word for word" is a post-literacy concept. Rubin & Wallace: meaning alone or rhyme alone fails; meaning+rhyme → ghost/steel/year almost always. Disassembly: 10 parts = 3.5M orderings but physical+cultural constraints (washers before nuts, screws clockwise) reduce to a few learnable choices.
- Ali Baba/Kasim: forgotten password = death. Password tyranny: most common passwords (password, 123456, qwerty…); the more complex the requirements, the LESS secure (people write them down; Post-It above desk; dedicated employees violate rules most); burglars check the phone directory for "Mr. and Mrs. Safe"; security experts propped the Google secure door with a brick ("make something too secure and it becomes less secure"); toilets inside the secure area. Best current answer: multi-factor — something you have + something you know (+duress password idea). Biometrics: once fooled, can't be changed. Key loggers make password strength irrelevant.
- STM/working memory: ~5-7 items (10-12 with rehearsal); practical design number: 3-5. Fragile — any distraction wipes it. Capacity is ITEMS (maybe pointers into LTM), not seconds. Mental multiplication fails because paper method assumes external memory. Mnemonics transform arbitrary → meaningful (digit-consonant table: 4194780135092770 → "a hearty breakfast meal has pancakes"); never design assuming such skill. DESIGN: never present critical info that disappears (angry users kick computers); nurses write on hands because auto-logout (privacy motive) fights interruption reality. Mitigate interference via MULTIPLE MODALITIES: visual doesn't clash with auditory/haptic → cars: spoken directions + seat/wheel vibration for lane warnings (driving is visual).
- LTM: slow in, slow out; sleep strengthens (rehearsal during sleep; can also embellish/falsify). Retrieval = RECONSTRUCTION, not playback → biases, false memories easily implanted, eyewitnesses unreliable. Organization/retrieval: tip-of-the-tongue; wrong retrieval blocks right one — do something else to reset. Stored-under-one-interpretation ≠ findable under another.
- Arbitrary vs meaningful memory: rote learning = bane (hard, and gives NO recovery hint when things go wrong; still dominates schools). Meaningful structure = conceptual model power. Sayeki motorcycle turn-signal: reinterpreted switch motion as HANDLEBAR motion → natural mapping → remembered forever. Design implications: provide meaningful structure, or better MAKE MEMORY UNNECESSARY (menus/GUI = knowledge in world).
- Approximate models (Whitehead: "civilization advances by extending the number of operations we can perform without thinking"): practice needs good-enough, not truth. °C=(°F−30)/2 beats the exact formula in the head. "5 slots, new item knocks one out" STM model: false but design-useful. Countersteering: riders' conceptual model is wrong, yet Sayeki's wrong model is USEFUL (and training must demonstrate the truth with the palm-push exercise).

## Ch3 (end)
- Good-enough arithmetic: 27×293 ≈ 30×300=9000 (−3×300 → 8100, within 2%); estimates in heads, precision in machines; "the unaided mind is surprisingly limited. It is THINGS that make us smart." Science seeks truth, practice needs approximations (scientists' disagreements are about details irrelevant to application).
- Pilots & ATC strings: write it down / enter into equipment AS HEARD / chunk as meaningful familiar patterns (expected altitudes, pre-set frequencies; "expect 3,000 in 10" = no action needed). Design implication: the easier to enter info into equipment as heard, the fewer memory errors; digital ATC delivery helps but kills party-line awareness of other pilots. 
- PROSPECTIVE MEMORY (remember to do X later) + memory for the future (planning). Reminder = SIGNAL (that something must be remembered) + MESSAGE (what it is). String-on-finger = signal only; note-to-self = message only; ideal has both, AT THE RIGHT TIME/PLACE (too early/late = useless). Book-against-the-door / keys-under-the-book tactic. The proliferation of reminder apps and paper diaries proves none fully works.
- Tradeoff table (3.1): world-knowledge = available when perceivable, interpretation substitutes for learning, first-use easy, but slow (find+interpret), can clutter and depends on environmental stability; head-knowledge = learning cost, retrieval effort, first-use hard, but fast when automated and lets the design look clean (minimalism trades beauty against learnability). "Out of sight, out of mind"; screens make world-knowledge invisible unless deliberately re-displayed.
- Transactive memory (Wegner): groups reconstruct ("Pampas… Churrascaria") by mutual constraint; "cybermind" = tech as same principle. Person+artifact = smart; alone minus calculator/GPS/contacts we're helpless — but that dependence is as old as housing and electricity; what matters is quality of the end result.
- Stove mapping: 4 burners in a rectangle + 4 controls in a row = guaranteed error (4 mappings all in commercial use; manufacturers won't standardize; best+worst in the same catalog). Natural mapping levels: BEST controls ON the thing; 2nd controls CLOSE to it; 3rd controls in the SAME SPATIAL CONFIGURATION (car window switches). Gesture faucets = right mapping, missing signifiers (wave-in-vain problem). Full natural mapping = relationship entirely in the world, zero memory load. TEST PRODUCTS BEFORE BUYING (pretend to cook in the store); purchaser ≠ user is a root obstacle.
- Culture & mapping: presentation-remote top button = forward? Audiences split worldwide — moving-person vs moving-image metaphor (= time cultures: person moves along time line vs time moves toward person; Aymara: future is BEHIND you (can't see it), past ahead; Arabic/Hebrew right-to-left time; Australian Aborigines: time flows east→west regardless of body). Scrolling: moving-window metaphor (mouse down = text up, 2 decades) vs moving-text (touchscreens; Apple switched everything); attitude indicator: outside-in vs inside-out. EVERY point of view is correct; design difficulty = metaphor SWITCHES (pilot retraining, road-side switches). Expect confusion when breaking convention, then people adapt.

## Ch4 Constraints, Discoverability, Feedback
- Lego motorcycle (15 pieces): assembly without instructions via FOUR universal constraint classes — PHYSICAL (peg/hole; windshield fits one place), CULTURAL (red light = brake = rear; blue = police top; yellow headlight = fading convention — cultural constraints change), SEMANTIC (meaning: rider faces forward, windshield protects face; also changes — what will brake lights mean among self-driving cars?), LOGICAL (one piece left, one place left; leftover faucet part = error; natural mappings are logical constraints).
- Physical constraints best when visible/interpretable BEFORE acting (else they only block after a failed try). Battery problem: cylindrical cells insertable both ways, black-on-black polarity marks; fixes = symmetric-terminal battery (exists, unused), Microsoft InstaLoad contacts (orientation-free; 2 years, zero adopters incl. Microsoft), shape-constrained cells. LEGACY PROBLEM named: standards lock in inelegance (change breaks the installed base). Keys/USB: orientation-insensitive designs exist, unused ("this is how we've always done it") — small neglect symptomatic of larger customer-disregard. Root-need thinking: we don't want better keys, we want authorized-only access → badges, biometrics, keyless.
- Cultural: schemas/scripts (Schank & Abelson), frames (Goffman); elevator-facing-backward experiment; conventions → standards/laws (traffic precedence; lane-merge alternation = informal); CLASHING conventions: Mexico headlight-flash = "I'm going first", England = "you go first" (experts warn against the signal entirely); dinner-utensil anxiety; violating conventions marks you a rude outsider.

## Ch4 (cont.)
- Doors: hardware itself should be the signifier (plate = push HERE; panic bar = affordance+constraint+signifier in one). Car OUTSIDE handles excellent (recessed slit guides hand: horizontal=pull, vertical=slide); inside handles unsolved. Cabinet push-to-open-outward latch = cleverness that punishes first-time users (glass door makes "push" look impossible). Paris Métro doors need passenger action — behavior right in one system is wrong in an identical-looking other; unknown norms = discomfort.
- Switches: auditorium fumble ritual; identical flap/landing-gear switches side by side → pilots retracting wheels on the ground (NTSB report: known principles ignored for 50 years). Two problems: WHAT does it control + WHICH one (mapping). Fixes: Norman's Del Mar floor-plan switch panel — switches placed ON a tilted floor plan of the room (tilt = see mapping + anti-affordance against coffee cups); parts don't exist commercially (he pitched the CEO and failed); future: wireless switches anywhere, but touchscreens lose elbow-operability (hands-full problem).
- ACTIVITY-CENTERED CONTROLS ("video"/"lecture" presets) > device-centered (separate screens for light/sound/projector = cognitive interruption); trap: EXCEPTIONS — raising lights mid-talk canceled the whole "talk" activity; manual override must not cancel the activity. Requires real task analysis.
- FORCING FUNCTIONS (physical constraints where failure at one stage blocks the next): car won't start without the token. Three safety types: INTERLOCK (proper sequence: microwave door cuts power; brake pedal before leaving Park; dead-man's switch — operators tie it down, so designs demand mid-pressure/repeated action/queries); LOCK-IN (keeps operation active: "save before exit?" prompt — so good Norman exits WITHOUT saving as his save shortcut; jail cells; business lock-in: proprietary ecosystems — everyone loses except the dominant manufacturer); LOCKOUT (keeps you out: stair gate at ground floor stops panic-fleeing into the basement; baby locks; fire-extinguisher pin). Design tension: nuisance in normal use → people disable safety features → minimize nuisance while keeping protection (the gate = enough restraint to notice, not enough to prop open). Restroom shelf that blocks the door until you take your bag = forcing function against forgetting.
- Perceived affordance → action meaning is learned via CONVENTION (doorknob opens doors only by culture; same knob on a wall ≠ open the wall).
- Destination-control elevators: better (grouping by floor = fewer stops, faster), but no in-cab floor buttons → designers wrote angry articles. "The merits of the new system are irrelevant: it is the CHANGE that is upsetting." Metric system: 200+ years, 3 holdout countries; switching cost feared, actual learning easy. RULE: consistency wins for marginal improvements; for VASTLY superior designs change is worth the disruption — but everybody must change together (mixed systems hurt everyone). Invented 1985, first install 1990, spread decades later.
- Faucet case history: conference pamphlet explaining TAPS = failure. Human needs = temperature+flow; plumbing = hot pipe+cold pipe (structure/need conflict). Mapping conventions: left=hot, right=cold (violated so often in England it's not a convention there); clockwise=off (screw thread); BLADE handles are conceptualized as push/pull not rotate → consistency must follow the PSYCHOLOGICAL conceptual model, not physical mechanics (mirror-image "symmetry" plumbing = scalding shower gropes). Single-control faucets control the right psychological variables but unstandardized dimensions/directions = 4 new mapping problems + invisible controls in the name of elegance. Shower feedback delay (5s of pipe) makes wrong-direction errors brutal. THE PRINCIPLE OF DESPERATION: "If all else fails, standardize" — standardize what must live in the head; standards = cultural constraint; they simplify life but hinder future development and are politically hard.
- SOUND AS SIGNIFIER: natural sounds carry source information (bolt click, tinny door, kettle, clogged vacuum pitch; mechanics diagnose by ear); beeps confirm but don't inform; absence of expected sound = missing feedback. Sound's virtue (attention-free) = its vice (intrusive, public).
- Silent EV case: electric cars kill pedestrians (NHTSA: more pedestrian hits, worst at low speed); blind people navigate by engine sound. Proposals: rocks in hubcaps (natural signal that self-fades at speed — brilliant), Porsche growl loudspeakers, tweeting birds, standardized sounds. SKEUOMORPHIC defined: new tech copying old forms (horseless carriage, wood-look plastic, folder icons) — purists decry it, but it eases transition by modifying rather than replacing conceptual models. EV sound criteria: ALERTING, ORIENTATION (location/speed/direction), NON-ANNOYANCE (unlike sirens: frequent sounds must be pleasant), STANDARDIZATION vs INDIVIDUALIZATION (interpretability vs tracking multiple cars + branding). NHTSA: required sound below 30 km/h, stationary-but-on, reversing. "After years making cars quieter, millions spent adding sound."

## Ch5 Human Error? No, Bad Design (start)
- 75-95% of industrial accidents blamed on "human error" — if 1-5% maybe believable; at that rate it's the DESIGN. Bridges collapse → redesign rules; people err → blame and continue. We design jobs requiring hours of vigilance, archaic procedures, boredom-then-sudden-response, interruptions + multitasking — then wonder at failure.

## Ch5 (cont.)
- Administrators admit their own dozing/wrong-burner errors yet blame workers for the same. "Blame and punish; blame and train" feels good, cures nothing — same error recurs. Determine WHY, then redesign product/procedure.
- Root cause analysis flaws: (1) accidents rarely have ONE cause (Reason's Swiss cheese); (2) analysis stops at "human error" — with machines we keep asking why the part broke; do the same for people. F-22 crash: AF blamed "pilot failed to recover"; Inspector General: pilot was probably UNCONSCIOUS (hypoxia). Parody: "So you agree, the pilot failed to correct the problem."
- FIVE WHYS (Toyoda/Toyota): keep asking why past the first answer; weaknesses: ambiguous whys, stopping at investigator's knowledge limit, single-cause bias.
- Utility-company electrocutions: committees stopped at worker fault (workers agreed! "I knew better" = not a valid analysis); fixes required culture change from "we are supermen, we don't err"; years later: "No changes. And we are still injuring people." If the system LETS you err = bad design; if it INDUCES the error = really bad design. Teaching won't stop the stove error; redesign will.
- Why people err: designs serve machine requirements (precision) not human ones (creativity, novelty-seeking, noticing change); TIME STRESS; "if operators obeyed all procedures the work would never get done" — boundary-pushing praised until it fails, then punished.
- DELIBERATE VIOLATIONS (≠ error; intentional known-risk deviations): routine (noncompliance so common it's ignored), situational (red light, empty road, late). Cause: rules written for legal compliance, not work reality; succeeding-by-violating gets rewarded → organizations train noncompliance.
- SLIPS vs MISTAKES (Norman & Reason taxonomy): SLIP = right goal, wrong execution (lower stages, subconscious); MISTAKE = wrong goal/plan (higher cognition). Slips: action-based (milk coffee into fridge) + memory-lapse (burner left on). Mistakes: rule-based, knowledge-based, memory-lapse (Gimli Glider: fuel in pounds not kg = knowledge-based; distracted mechanic = memory-lapse). Map to 7 stages: mistakes top 3, slips bottom 4, memory lapses at the 8 transitions.
- PARADOX: slips hit EXPERTS more than novices (automatic execution, attention elsewhere); novices' conscious attention prevents slips. Wristwatch-unbuckled-instead-of-seatbelt story.
- Slip types for design:
  - CAPTURE slip: more-frequent/recent sequence captures a shared-prefix action ("…8, 9, 10, Jack, Queen, King"); design rule: make sequences diverge FROM THE START; experts most vulnerable.
  - DESCRIPTION-SIMILARITY slip: right action, wrong similar object (sweaty shirt into toilet); vague internal description + similar targets; design: make controls/displays for different purposes LOOK AND FEEL different — cockpit shape-coding (flap lever shaped like a flap, gear lever like a wheel).
  - MEMORY-LAPSE slips: original left in copier; child left behind; ATM card — forcing-function fix: card must be removed BEFORE money (people don't forget the goal); minimize steps, vivid reminders; interruptions are the main cause.
  - MODE-ERROR slips: same control, different meanings per state (alarm set to 7 PM; entertainment system; Airbus: -3.3 entered as vertical speed (-3300 ft/min) instead of flight-path angle (-3.3°) → fatal; fix: 4-digit vs 2-digit display). Mode error IS design error: make the mode visible; avoid modes; compensate for interruptions.
- Mistake classes (Rasmussen skill/rule/knowledge behavior levels): RULE-BASED — wrong situation classification, faulty rule, or wrong evaluation (Kiss nightclub fire: flares indoors; guards blocking exits per no-pay rule → rule lacking emergency case; fix: alarmed exit doors. Oven-max thermostat = false model + memory lapse → fire. Anti-lock brakes vibration read as malfunction → foot off pedal). Matching to past is biased by recency/regularity/uniqueness; novel events get force-matched to old patterns. Hindsight is always superior to foresight: in the moment it's all noise (plants have thousands of standing minor faults; the one-in-a-million miss gets condemned retrospectively). Design: show system state coherently, graphically; offer alternative interpretations.
  - KNOWLEDGE-BASED: novel situation, no rules → conscious reflective problem-solving; needs good conceptual model, cooperative tools; automation does the easy parts and fails exactly when it gets complex (humans get the leftovers).
  - MEMORY-LAPSE mistakes: forgetting goal/plan/evaluation after interruption → keep goals/plans/current state continuously visible; don't erase signs of them once acted on.
- SOCIAL & INSTITUTIONAL PRESSURES: wrong diagnosis locks interpretation until fresh eyes (team turnover) arrive; money pressure keeps unsafe systems running. Tenerife 1977 (583 dead): KLM took off without clearance — delay+fog+junior first officer unable to override revered captain; Pan Am questioned taxi orders but obeyed. Air Florida 1982: ice on wings + 1.5h delay; first officer said "something's not right" FOUR times; captain ignored. Scuba instructor's counter-design: publicly praise + free replacement for anyone who drops expensive weights for safety (removes cost+embarrassment pressure). Design alone insufficient: train, reward safety over economics, make dangers visible.
- CHECKLISTS: cut slips+lapses; collaborative two-person read-execute beats solo-then-check (checker complacency); MORE checkers = LESS care (diffusion of responsibility paradox); aviation accepted them, medicine resists ("insult to competence" — no: to err is human; senior use ENHANCES authority); design checklists iteratively with designers+SMEs; printed lists force sequence → skipped-item lapses (fuel not ready yet); electronic checklists track skips and block completion until all done. Don't impose sequential structure unless the task requires it.

## Ch5 (end)
- REPORTING error: punishment/stigma suppresses the data needed to improve; thank reporters. Toyota JIDOKA ("automation with a human touch"): worker pulls the andon cord, line stops, experts converge, Five Whys to root cause; NOT reporting is what gets punished. POKA-YOKE (Shingo, "error proofing"): simple jigs/fixtures that make wrong operations impossible (asymmetric screw holes; Norman's green dots next to keyholes = added signifiers; switch covers = forcing function). NASA ASRS: semi-anonymous pilot self-reports, contact slip returned (NASA can't identify reporter), self-report = immunity from minor penalties; works because NASA is neutral with no enforcement power; medicine has no equivalent (license/lawsuit fear).
- DETECTING error: action slips easiest (visible discrepancy — IF there's feedback); memory-lapse slips hard (nothing happened = nothing to see); mistakes hardest — actions are CONSISTENT with the wrong goal, so monitoring adds false confidence; misdiagnoses are reasonable and initially helpful → undetected for hours/days. Explaining away anomalies: Las Vegas billboards on the "road to Mammoth" (missed turn, 4 wasted hours); most anomalies ARE innocent; distinguishing true from apparent anomaly is the hard part. Fischhoff hindsight studies: outcomes look obvious/predictable only AFTER; foresight runs at chance. Ignore journalists' instant accident narratives; wait for NTSB-grade reports.
- DESIGNING FOR ERROR (conversation model: humans treat each other's errors as approximations — clarify, don't beep): understand causes & minimize; SENSIBILITY CHECKS (common-sense test: radiation dose 1000x, won-vs-dollar million-dollar transfer — query abnormal magnitudes); make actions REVERSIBLE / make irreversible ones hard; make errors easy to discover & correct; treat action as approximation to intent, help complete it.
- Novices make MISTAKES, experts make SLIPS. Mistakes stem from ambiguous state info, no good model, bad procedures, poor feedback.
- INTERRUPTIONS: cost = handling time + RESUMPTION (recall goal, position in action cycle, system state); systems discard exactly that info. Multitasking is a myth: two tasks > sum of their times; hands-free phone degrades driving; walkers on phones missed the unicycling clown. FAA Sterile Cockpit below critical phases (side effect: attendants fail to report emergencies); Norman's household version: no talk during highway merges.
- Warning signals: all beep alike, all at once in emergencies, mostly tell you what you know → people disable them (then forget to re-enable = lapse). Design: attract attention AND convey the event; prioritize; coordinate across instruments (needs standards). Speech warnings: precise but collide with noise/conversation/other warnings.
- Error-blocking constraints: car fluids (segregated filling points, different shapes/sizes/colors = anti-description-similarity); electronics: segregate confusable controls, modularize (irrelevant controls off-screen).
- UNDO = the single most powerful mitigation; multi-level; amazing how many systems omit it. Confirmations are ill-timed ("delete my most important file" joke): focus the person on the OBJECT not the action (show both + cancel/do-it); against SLIPS confirmations work (surprise alerts you), against MISTAKES they're ignored (expected dialog, auto-accepted — the wrong-window close that loses work: lapse + 2 mistakes). Designer fixes: make the acted-on object prominent; make the operation reversible (secretly save on "don't save").
- Minimizing slips: do NOT demand constant conscious attention (skilled behavior is subconscious and should stay so); make actions/controls dissimilar and far apart; kill modes or make them loudly visible; best = perceptible feedback on action + resulting state + undo. Med-barcode scanning (prescription↔medication↔wristband) = huge wrong-drug reduction at tiny workload cost. Common practice reads like a recipe FOR slips: identical control rows, hidden modes, interruption-blind designs, no reminders for rare procedures (capture). "Good design can prevent slips and mistakes. Design can save lives."
- SWISS CHEESE (Reason): accident = holes in ALL slices lined up; "the" cause doesn't exist ("if only" statements are all true, none is the cause); defenses: more slices (layers of defense/checklists), fewer+smaller holes (better design), alert when holes line up. NTSB chair Hersman: "design redundancy and layers of defense." Investigate systems, not culprits.
- When people ARE at fault: sleep deprivation, drugs/alcohol, lack of training/licensure, deliberate gambles (one-in-a-million × 9M flights = 9 incidents; the three-low-oil-pressure pilot who was "right" about the odds and still the one). Hospitals ignore the sleep rules airlines enforce. But the far greater share of accidents is still design.
- RESILIENCE ENGINEERING: design+test+improve equipment/procedures/communication continuously; deliberately break live systems to test recovery (chaos-style); "safety shows itself only by the events that do not happen"; resilience = creating FORESIGHT, anticipating changing risk.
- PARADOX OF AUTOMATION: takes the dull tasks, gives up on complex ones — exactly when needed most; failures come without warning, human is OUT OF THE LOOP; airplanes give minutes + trained pilots, cars give fractions of a second to amateurs; fewer accidents but BIGGER ones. Royal Majesty grounding: GPS antenna cable loose → silent switch to dead reckoning ("dr" in tiny letters) → days of unnoticed drift; years of flawless automation had built trust that killed the manual checks (giant mode error).
- Final error-design principles: knowledge in the world (but allow expert speed); constraints (all 4 kinds) + forcing functions + natural mappings; bridge both gulfs — FEEDFORWARD (options visible) + FEEDBACK (results apparent; state readable in goal-consistent form). Embrace error; assist rather than punish.

## Ch6 Design Thinking (start)
- Norman's consulting rule: NEVER solve the problem as given — it's a symptom. Students solve brilliantly without asking "is this the right problem?" Engineers/business solve problems; designers DISCOVER the real problem. A brilliant solution to the wrong problem is worse than none.
- Design thinking = resist jumping to solutions; find root issue, consider wide solution range, then converge. Tools: HCD + double diamond.
- DOUBLE DIAMOND (British Design Council 2005): diamond 1 = find the RIGHT PROBLEM (diverge-discover, converge-define); diamond 2 = find the RIGHT SOLUTION (diverge-develop, converge-deliver). Managers hate the divergence ("going backward").

## Ch6 (cont.)
- Double diamond in practice: designers question the assignment, travel, half-form impractical ideas, sometimes discover mid-convergence that the problem was wrong and restart (faster 2nd time). Manager's tool: encourage free exploration BUT hold schedule/budget — "nothing like a firm deadline to get creative minds to converge."
- HCD cycle: OBSERVATION → IDEATION → PROTOTYPING → TESTING, iterated (spiral). 
  - Observation = design research/applied ethnography: watch people in their natural environment (homes, commutes, bars; "follow them into the shower"); match observed people to the TARGET population by ACTIVITIES not demographics (activities are similar worldwide; subcultures like Japanese teen girls need targeted study when product = fashion/status); for foreign markets GO THERE with natives on the team.
  - Design research vs market research: design = what people NEED and how they'll USE it (qualitative, deep, tens of people); marketing = what people BUY (quantitative, focus groups, surveys, big data/A-B tests). Big data trap: correlations say nothing of needs/desires/reasons but seduce executives with sexy charts. Both needed: buying AND using go in the spec.
  - Ideation rules: many ideas; no criticism/constraints yet; + Norman's rule: QUESTION EVERYTHING, especially "stupid" questions (the obvious is just what's never questioned).
  - Prototyping: sketches, cardboard, PowerPoint, skits; WIZARD OF OZ (human behind the curtain simulates the system — PARC airline-reservation study: people's language needed world knowledge + problem solving, not just parsing; goal changed from understanding sentences to understanding ACTIVITIES). Prototype in diamond 1 to understand the problem, in diamond 2 to test solutions.
  - Testing: target users, use as they actually would; PAIRS thinking aloud (one drives, one interprets) surfaces hypotheses/frustrations naturally; retrace steps afterwards with video. Jakob Nielsen's FIVE: test 5, refine, iterate — multiple improvement cycles beat one big test.
  - Iteration = "fail frequently, fail fast" (Kelley/IDEO). Requirements made in the abstract are invariably wrong; requirements from ASKING people are invariably wrong; only watching works — people deviate from their own descriptions ("this was a special case" — MOST cases are special; systems that don't allow special cases fail).
- Activity-centered design (for global/heterogeneous populations): design around the ACTIVITY's conceptual model (cars identical worldwide; people accept learning complexity that feels essential to the activity — driving's many subtasks; iPod won by supporting the WHOLE activity: discover→buy→load→playlists→listen→ecosystem). Task < activity hierarchy: be-goals → do-goals → motor-goals (Carver & Scheier; Hassenzahl applies to UX). Design for tasks = too restrictive; design for activities = usable by everyone.
- Iterative vs waterfall/gated: iteration best early and for refining the problem; waterfall scales to huge projects + management control at gates (slow, weeks lost scheduling execs); best practice = iterate INSIDE the stages between gates; delay rigid specs until prototypes tested; even big systems can prototype (scale models, 3D prints, renders, VR). Large-project killers: management/communication across disciplines + long horizons (requirements/tech/people change; implicit knowledge walks out the door).
- "What I just told you? It doesn't really work that way": real product drivers = match competitors' features + chase new tech, not human needs. NORMAN'S LAW OF PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT: "The day a product development process starts, it is behind schedule and above budget." The "next time we'll do it right" lie repeats forever. FIX: separate design research from product teams — researchers ALWAYS in the field, so when the team launches the findings already exist (same for market research). Multidisciplinary teams with ALL disciplines present from day one (each speaks its own language, each thinks the others stupid; skilled product manager creates mutual respect); piecemeal after-the-fact changes by each downstream division wreck cohesion.
- Design challenge: clients ≠ end users (appliances bought by developers/landlords; copiers by purchasing on price+features — usability/training/maintenance not in the RFP → unusable office equipment; study the PURCHASER too). Internal groups (engineering/marketing/manufacturing/service) are also users — of the design team's output.
- No average person: anthropometry 95th percentile excludes 15M Americans; 99th excludes 70M worldwide; can't average a left- and right-hander → build VERSIONS; flexibility (adjustable everything) is the best answer.
- STIGMA: devices for infirmities get rejected ("I'd have to be around all those old people" — a 95-year-old). OXO peeler: designed for an arthritic wife, marketed as BETTER FOR EVERYONE → revolutionized the category; the arthritis virtue was never advertised and the right users still found it. Canes were once FASHION. Make assistive devices things of pride. Aging: vision adjusts poorly from mid-40s; old age keeps/improves experience-based wisdom while agility/attention-division decline; design teams benefit from the mix. Inclusive/universal design helps everyone (bigger type, adjustability).
- COMPLEXITY IS GOOD; CONFUSION IS BAD (Living with Complexity): someone else's kitchen is confusing, yours isn't — confusion lives in the MIND, not the kitchen; tools must match the complexity of tasks; conceptual models tame complexity ("complex things are no longer complicated once understood").

## Ch6 (end) + Ch7 Design in the World of Business
- Standardization = cultural constraint + usability breakthrough (learn one car, drive any). Standards process: 5+ years, politicized (incumbents gain advantage), compromises (sometimes several incompatible standards: metric/English, plugs, voltages). Nonstandard clock demo: same logic, unreadable — without standards you'd recompute the mapping every time. HDTV saga: 35 years Japanese broadcast→US adoption; computer vs TV industry war over 720p/1080i; "lock yourselves in a room"; aspect ratio 16:9 chosen because engineers liked 4:3 squared; standards can take so long technology overruns them — but they're still necessary. Don't standardize too early (locked into primitive tech) or too late (installed bases). Decimal time (France 1792, Swatch .beats 1998) never took: habits too strong.
- DELIBERATELY DIFFICULT design (Kurktchi's question): school door for handicapped children needing two distant latches = GOOD design. Things that SHOULD be hard: doors keeping people in/out, security, dangerous equipment/operations, secret compartments, routine-disrupting confirmations (gun safeties, extinguisher pins), two-person controls, child-proof caps, GAMES (designers deliberately flout usability — figuring out what to do IS the challenge). Even then: only the one gate is hard, the rest follows good design; and the rules used in REVERSE tell you how (hide things, unnatural mappings both directions, physical difficulty, precise timing, no feedback). Safety features create secondary dangers (hole barriers need their own lights).
- Ch7 competitive forces: price, features, quality — "unfortunately often in that order". Zeitgeist: ideas in the air (startup's cooking equipment matched at trade shows without theft). Real customers are often DISTRIBUTORS, not users. Companies prohibit designers from meeting customers (leak fear + Osborne effect) = bizarre.
- FEATURITIS / creeping featurism: pressure from existing customers wanting more, competitors' features, saturated markets needing upgrade reasons; no budget ever to REMOVE features; Lego motorcycle went 15 pieces (no instructions) → 29 pieces (instructions required). Youngme Moon (Different): feature-matching makes all products identical — ignore the competition's checklist, strengthen your STRENGTHS and market those; being "good enough" elsewhere is fine. Bezos "customer obsessed": focus on customers, ignore competition — usually only survives while the founder runs the company; MBA profit-first → quality decay → desertion.
- New tech forces change (phones/keyboards century view): devices merged into "smart screens"; word-gesture typing (Swype); Zhai's optimized non-QWERTY layout failed on the LEGACY problem. Fundamental needs persist (writing, stories); reading ~300 wpm (skimmable, thousands effective) vs listening ~60 wpm (serial) → speech gets transcribed. Technology changes HOW; psychology stays constant, so the design rules persist.
- Time-to-acceptance: idea→product months, product→success DECADES. Rule of thumb: 20 years lab→commercial product + 1-2 decades commercial→widespread. Most innovations fail entirely; too-early excellent ideas fail and succeed later under others (Apple QuickTake first digital camera; first digital photo frame; Duryea car; Altair). Multitouch: Toronto early-80s → Mitsubishi lab product → Fingerworks nearly died → Apple bought it → iPhone ~30 years after invention. Videophone: drawn 1878 (du Maurier/"Edison's Telephonoscope" — Stigler's law: credit goes to the capitalizer not originator), working 1920s, first product 1930s Germany (failed), US 1960s (failed), common only 2010s — 140 years. Concept is easy; DETAILS and manufacturable components are the hard part.
- QWERTY history: Sholes 1870s; layout to keep colliding typebars apart (NOT to slow typists — actually speeds typing via hand alternation of frequent pairs); possible marketing hack (R and P moved so "typewriter" types on one row — unconfirmed); keyboard slope/diagonals = mechanical linkage leftovers kept in electronics. Legacy momentum beats efficiency.
